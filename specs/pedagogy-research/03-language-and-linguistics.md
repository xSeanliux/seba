# Effective 1:1 Teaching of (A) a Foreign Language and (B) Linguistics

Two different problems. (A) is skill acquisition under processing constraints — the bottleneck is automatization and noticing. (B) is conceptual/analytical learning — the bottleneck is argument construction from data. Evidence and tutor design differ accordingly.

---

# PART A — Second language acquisition, tutoring-relevant

## A1. Input is necessary, not sufficient

**Claim.** Krashen's Input Hypothesis (comprehensible input at i+1, acquisition as subconscious, the Affective Filter) remains the correct floor — you cannot acquire what you never comprehend — but its strong form ("learners acquire in only one way") is not supported. The i+1 construct is unoperationalized (no independent measure of "i", so no way to compute "+1"), the Monitor/acquisition-learning split is unfalsifiable as stated, and instructed learners outperform uninstructed ones on accuracy, which pure-input theory does not predict.
- Critical reviews: [jehd.thebrpi.org critical review (PDF)](http://jehd.thebrpi.org/journals/jehd/Vol_4_No_4_December_2015/16.pdf); [Frontiers in Psychology (2025), neuro-ecological critique](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1636777/full) — argues acquisition is "constantly active, brain-modifying," requiring interaction and feedback, and that one-size i+1 fails against adaptive/personalized systems.

**Tutor implication.** Never build a tutor on input alone. But *do* keep the coverage constraint (A6) as the operational replacement for "i+1", since it is measurable and i+1 is not.

## A2. Interaction and negotiation of meaning

**Claim.** Long's Interaction Hypothesis: conversational adjustment (clarification requests, confirmation checks, comprehension checks) makes input comprehensible *and* delivers negative evidence at the exact moment of communicative breakdown. Mackey & Goo's meta-analysis of 28 interaction studies found **large effect sizes for interaction vs. no-interaction, with effects clearer on delayed posttests** than immediate ones — i.e., interaction produces durable, not just momentary, gains.
- [Mackey & Goo (2007), Interaction research in SLA: a meta-analysis](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Interaction-research-in-SLA-%3A-A-meta-analysis-and-Mackey-Goo/2b270feebe31f2f01e7c645cc00f8e94d504fb8b); [Mackey, Interaction Approach (Wiley)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781405198431.wbeal0551)

**Tutor implication.** A dialogic LLM tutor is *structurally* the right instrument — it is a tireless interlocutor who can negotiate. The delayed-posttest advantage argues for conversation over drills as the backbone. What text chat loses is prosody/paralinguistic disambiguation (see A4); what it gains is a persistent visible record, which supports delayed FonF (A3).

## A3. Focus on Form (FonF) vs. focus on formS vs. focus on meaning

**Definitions (Long 1988/1991/1997).** *FonF* = primarily meaning-focused interaction with brief, often incidental attention shifted to a linguistic form when it is needed for communication. *FonFS* = forms taught in isolation as a synthetic syllabus (Monday: the subjunctive). *Focus on meaning* = no attention to form at all.
- [Long (1997), Focus on form in TBLT (PDF)](https://woucentral.weebly.com/uploads/7/4/6/9/7469707/long_1997_intro_focus_on_form.pdf); [Loewen, Focus on Form vs Focus on Forms (Wiley)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9781118784235.eelt0062)

**Rationale.** Three psycholinguistic legs: (i) attention/noticing is necessary for uptake; (ii) learners follow an internal syllabus — developmental sequences resist reordering by teaching; (iii) instruction works best at developmental readiness. FonF gains two things over pure meaning focus: it increases the significance of positive evidence, and it supplies negative evidence, which naturalistic input cannot.

**The awkward evidence.** Norris & Ortega's meta-analysis (49 studies, 1980–1998) found focused L2 instruction produces large, durable gains; **explicit instruction outperformed implicit; and FonF and FonFS produced roughly *equivalent* large effects** — contra the strong FonF claim.
- [Norris & Ortega (2000), Language Learning](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/0023-8333.00136); [ERIC EJ611436](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ611436); revisited: [Goo et al., Norris & Ortega revisited and updated](https://benjamins.com/catalog/sibil.48.18goo)

Caveat the field itself raises: Norris & Ortega's outcome measures skewed toward discrete-point, metalinguistic tests, which structurally favor FonFS. Lyster & Saito (below) found feedback effects "most apparent in measures that elicit free constructed responses" — i.e., the measure decides the winner.

**Tutor implication.** Don't read "FonF beats grammar drilling" as settled — it isn't, on paper tests. Read it as: *the measurement you care about is free production, and on free production the meaning-embedded, feedback-driven route wins.* Practical synthesis: run meaning-primary tasks, insert **explicit** form attention reactively when the form blocks or degrades the message, then return to meaning. Explicit ≠ decontextualized.

## A4. Corrective feedback — the single most implementable finding

**Taxonomy (Lyster & Ranta 1997)**, from 18.3 hours of French immersion classroom transcript, 686 error-treatment sequences:

| Type | Definition | Share of teacher feedback | Repair? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Recast** | Teacher reformulates the error correctly, meaning preserved | **55%** (375/686) | Weakest: **260/375 no uptake at all**; only **66/375 → uptake with repair (<20%)**; ~70% went unnoticed |
| **Elicitation** | Pauses for completion, asks reformulation, asks a question forcing the form | 14% | Strong |
| **Clarification request** | "Sorry?" / "I don't understand" | 11% | Strong |
| **Metalinguistic feedback** | Comment/info/question about the form, no correct form given | 8% | Strong |
| **Explicit correction** | Names the error and supplies the form | 7% | Supplies form, low push |
| **Repetition** | Repeats the error, often with marked intonation | 5% | Strong |

The four middle types Lyster groups as **prompts** ("negotiation of form") — they withhold the correct form and push the learner to produce it. That is the operative distinction: **recasts and explicit correction *give* the form; prompts *demand* it.**
- [Lyster & Ranta (1997), SSLA 19:37–66](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/CORRECTIVE-FEEDBACK-AND-LEARNER-UPTAKE-Lyster-Ranta/eecbea6c9fdc3a79deb060123883a6de920c70fc); [Russell (2009) review, e-FLT 6(1):21–31 (PDF)](https://l2aquisition.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/corrective-feedback-over-a-decade-of-research-since-lyster-and-ranta-1997-where-do-we-stand-today.pdf)

**Effectiveness meta-analyses.**
- **Li (2010)**, 33 studies: overall CF effect **d = 0.64** (medium), maintained over time. Explicit feedback showed larger *immediate* effects; **implicit feedback was better maintained on long-delayed posttests**. Lab > classroom; shorter treatments > longer; FL contexts > SL contexts. [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00561.x) · [ERIC EJ883422](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ883422)
- **Lyster & Saito (2010)**, 15 classroom studies, 827 learners: CF significantly aids development; **effects larger for prompts than recasts**; largest on free-constructed-response measures; younger learners benefited more; instructional setting was not a significant moderator. [ERIC EJ892626](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ892626) · [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/ORAL-FEEDBACK-IN-CLASSROOM-SLA-Lyster-Saito/3b641377dc6657f21f555d964a635480ec854cd7)

**Why recasts fail, and when they don't.** Lyster (1998b): learners perceive recasts as *confirmation of meaning*, not correction — ambiguous because they sit in discourse alongside non-corrective repetitions. But this is context-dependent, and the counter-evidence is substantial: Oliver & Mackey (2003) found **85% uptake of recasts in explicitly language-focused contexts**; Sheen (2004) found EFL-Korea and ESL-NZ learners took up recasts far more than French immersion learners; Han (2002) and Iwashita (2003) found intensive recasting drives noticing, arguing recasts are *more salient* than other positive evidence. Philp (2003) identified the constraints: recasts of **five or fewer morphemes** were recalled far more accurately; recall dropped sharply with **more than two changes** in a single recast; higher-proficiency learners recalled recasts better than low. Carpenter et al. (2006): learners who did not see the triggering error could not distinguish recasts from mere repetitions. Mackey & Philp (1998) warn that immediate uptake is a "methodological red herring" — it does not equal learning.
- Sources as in [Russell (2009)](https://l2aquisition.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/corrective-feedback-over-a-decade-of-research-since-lyster-and-ranta-1997-where-do-we-stand-today.pdf)

**Error-type matching (Lyster 1998b).** Teachers used negotiation of form for **lexical** errors and recasts for **phonological and grammatical** ones — but negotiation of form produced more repair for grammatical and lexical errors, and recasts produced more repair for **phonological** errors. Lyster's recommendation: use prompts, not recasts, for grammar.

**Timing.** Mixed but leaning immediate: of 20 studies reviewed, **half favored immediate feedback** on posttests, delayed posttests, or both; none of the systematic reviews report a clean delayed-feedback advantage. In *text-based CMC specifically* — the closest analogue to an LLM tutor — immediate/synchronous feedback is repeatedly favored (Shintani 2015; Shintani & Aubrey 2016; Arroyo & Yilmaz 2017, 2018; Yilmaz & Sağdıç 2019). Shintani (2015): synchronous CF produced more successful self-correction, and crucially **meaning and form were attended contiguously** under synchronous feedback but separately under asynchronous.
- [Optimal timing of treatment for errors: systematic review (PMC9995700)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9995700/); [The timing of corrective feedback in L2 learning, Language Teaching (Cambridge)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/timing-of-corrective-feedback-in-second-language-learning/0E8856852D0183E9DD91EDB4C249E245)

**Tutor implication — the core algorithm.** Default to **prompts** (clarification request → elicitation → metalinguistic cue), escalating to explicit correction only if two prompts fail. Reserve recasts for (a) phonological/spelling-level errors, (b) forms above the learner's current level where a prompt would just fail, and (c) moments when protecting flow matters more than the form. If recasting, obey Philp's constraints: **≤5 morphemes, ≤2 changes, one target per recast** — and in text, mark the change (bold/caret) since you have no intonation to lean on and text kills the paralinguistic cue channel Carpenter et al. showed learners don't use anyway. Correct immediately and inline; hold non-blocking errors for an end-of-turn "form note" so the meaning thread survives.

## A5. Output, pushed output, and language-related episodes

**Claim.** Swain's Output Hypothesis: production forces syntactic processing (comprehension can succeed on semantics alone), and serves three functions — **noticing the gap**, **hypothesis testing**, and **metalinguistic reflection**. Output must be *pushed*: pressed toward accuracy and appropriateness, not merely elicited.
- [Swain (1995/2000), Output hypothesis and beyond](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/284106490_The_output_hypothesis_and_beyond_Mediating_acquisition_through_collaborative_dialogue); [JALT review of Swain's approach](https://jalt-publications.org/tlt/articles/2198-output-and-beyond-dialogue-review-merrill-swains-current-approach-sla)

**Dictogloss and LREs.** Dictogloss (hear a short text at normal speed, take notes, reconstruct it collaboratively) reliably generates **language-related episodes** — stretches of talk where learners consciously discuss a form. LaPierre (1994), using dyad-specific posttest items built from each pair's own recorded dialogue, found **evidence of learning in ~80% of 140 metalinguistic episodes**. LREs are not off-task; they *are* the acquisition event.
- [LREs research (Academy Publication, PDF)](https://www.academypublication.com/issues/past/jltr/vol04/03/04.pdf)

**Tutor implication.** Dictogloss is nearly free in text and is one of the highest-value 1:1 activities: present a 3–5 sentence passage, remove it, have the learner reconstruct, then compare reconstruction against original **jointly and clause by clause** — the comparison step is the LRE generator. Same logic for L1→L2 translation of short passages: it forces every form, allows no avoidance, and produces a clean diff. Log the specific forms surfaced in LREs — those are the learner's live acquisition frontier and the correct spaced-review queue.

## A6. Difficulty calibration — replace i+1 with coverage

**Claim.** Hu & Nation (2000): L2 readers need roughly **98% known lexical coverage** for adequate unassisted comprehension of a narrative; the study manipulated a 633-word text to 80/90/95/100% coverage with pseudowords, 66 learners. **98% = one unknown word in 50.** 95% is the commonly cited floor for assisted/instructed comprehension. Kremmel et al. (2023) replicated and **could not fully reproduce** the 98% threshold across different populations — treat 95–98% as a band, not a constant, and note the original's n=66.
- [Kremmel et al. (2023), Language Learning replication](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lang.12622); [Conti's review of the 95–98% evidence](https://gianfrancoconti.com/2025/02/27/why-the-input-we-give-our-learners-must-be-95-98-comprehensible-in-order-to-enhance-language-acquisition-the-theory-and-the-research-evidence/)

**Tutor implication.** This is the *operationalizable* i+1. Generate input targeting ~2 unknown items per 50 words when the learner is reading unaided, ~5 per 100 when you are present to gloss. An LLM tutor can hit this exactly — and its natural failure is to overshoot into fluent native-like prose. Track the known-word list and target it deliberately.

## A7. Vocabulary: exposures, chunks, narrow input

- **Repetitions.** Nation & Wang (1999): words are likely to be learned after ~10 encounters; Pigada & Schmitt (2006): substantial gains at 10+; reliable learning of *multiple* aspects of a word (form, meaning, collocation, constraints) needs **~8–10 meetings**. [Principles guiding vocabulary learning through extensive reading (ERIC PDF)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1059712.pdf); [Extensive reading and incidental vocabulary acquisition (ERIC PDF)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1219495.pdf)
- **Narrow reading/listening (Krashen).** Reading several texts by one author or on one topic recycles vocabulary and syntax naturally, so the 8–10 encounters accumulate without artificial drilling, and each recurrence appears in a *different* context — which is what builds the full lexical entry rather than a translation pair. Cho & Krashen (1994, 1995) documented substantial vocabulary growth and high motivation from single-series reading. [Krashen, The Case for Narrow Reading (PDF)](https://www.sdkrashen.com/content/articles/narrow.pdf); [How narrow reading leads to advanced L2 proficiency (ERIC PDF)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1225871.pdf)
- **Formulaic sequences.** **50–80% of native speech and writing is formulaic** — collocations, chunks, fixed frames, processed as single units. Boers et al. (2006) found chunk use predicts rated oral proficiency; chunks reduce cognitive load during message construction, freeing capacity for the parts that need composing. [Boers et al. (2006), Language Teaching Research](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1191/1362168806lr195oa); [Formulaic sequences and implications for SLA (ERIC PDF)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1103454.pdf)

**Tutor implication.** Store and review **chunks with their frame**, not bare words (`avere voglia di + inf.`, not `voglia = desire`). Engineer the 8–10 encounters through narrow input — keep a topic/author thread running across sessions rather than a fresh topic each time. Every re-encounter should be in a new context; a word met 10 times in the same sentence is met once.

## A8. Spacing, interleaving, retrieval, production

**Claim.** Spacing across days beats massing the same total time. Interleaving grammatical categories (rather than blocking one tense per session) improves discrimination between confusable structures — demonstrated for both *similar and dissimilar* Romance tenses, and it improves identification of which language a sentence belongs to. Both spacing and interleaving **slow apparent learning during practice while improving retention and transfer** — the classic desirable-difficulty signature, which means learner-perceived difficulty is not a valid signal to back off. Retrieval practice specifically closes the receptive-productive gap.
- [Interleaved practice enhances grammar skill learning for similar and dissimilar tenses (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959475224001725); [Effects of spaced practice on L2 learning: a meta-analysis](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/358406370_The_Effects_of_Spaced_Practice_on_Second_Language_Learning_A_Meta-Analysis); [MIT Open Learning, spaced and interleaved practice](https://openlearning.mit.edu/mit-faculty/research-based-learning-findings/spaced-and-interleaved-practice)

**Tutor implication.** Never run a "past subjunctive session." Mix the two or three structures the learner actually confuses in one session, and always test by production, not recognition — multiple choice measures something the learner cannot do in conversation. Warn the learner explicitly that interleaved practice will feel worse and work better, or they will ask you to block it.

## A9. Affect: anxiety, WTC, and how not to shut the learner down

**Claim.** Willingness to Communicate (MacIntyre et al.) is the last psychological gate before actual L2 use; foreign language anxiety reliably suppresses it and is **self-exacerbating** — anxiety builds on itself. **The harshness of error correction is a documented input to FLA.** Anxiety and enjoyment are negatively correlated but largely independent dimensions, so reducing anxiety is not the same lever as increasing enjoyment — you need both. The literature's own recommendation: frame errors as evidence of learning and correct **unobtrusively**. Morris & Tarone (2003) found negative social dynamics distort how learners perceive even well-intended recasts — corrections read as mockery when the relationship is wrong.
- [FLA, grit and WTC (PMC9516278)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9516278/); [FLA and boredom predicting WTC (PMC10558861)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10558861/); [Affect: language anxiety and other emotions (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1057/9781137032829_8)

**Tutor implication — the central tension.** A4 says push hard with prompts; A9 says pushing raises anxiety and anxiety kills output. Resolution: **make the correction rate a budget, not a reflex.** Correct one target form per turn, chosen by (i) does it block comprehension, (ii) is it in the current teaching focus, (iii) is it a repeated error — and let everything else go silently. Text tutors have a specific advantage here (no live audience, no time pressure, learner controls pace) and a specific hazard (a wall of red is far more visible than a spoken correction and reads as a verdict).

## A10. Tasks (TBLT) and pre-task planning

**Claim.** Tasks — outcome-oriented, meaning-primary, resembling real use — are the unit of syllabus in TBLT (Ellis, Skehan, Long). Pre-task **strategic planning** (deciding what to say before performing) reliably improves **complexity and fluency**, with much weaker effects on accuracy; **rehearsal** (performing the task once, then repeating it) is the variant that also lifts accuracy. Task design features systematically shift the complexity/accuracy/fluency tradeoff — e.g. decision-making tasks elicit more complex language than information-exchange tasks.
- [Ellis, Task preparedness (Benjamins)](https://benjamins.com/catalog/tblt.13.02ell); [Ellis, TBLT: sorting out the misunderstandings (PDF)](https://philliria.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2010/12/rod-ellis-task-based-language-teaching-sorting-out-the-misunderstandings.pdf); [Pre-task planning in L2 text-chat (ScholarSpace)](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/bad80397-26cd-4b3c-862e-46b537c86ff9/content)

**Tutor implication.** You cannot maximize complexity, accuracy, and fluency simultaneously — pick one per task and tell the learner which. Task repetition is the cheapest accuracy lever available in a chat tutor: run the same task twice, once cold and once after form feedback, and the second pass is where accuracy gains show.

## A11. LLM/chatbot tutors: what the evidence actually says

**Positive.** Meta-analyses and systematic reviews find chatbots improve **vocabulary acquisition, speaking fluency, writing quality, and motivation**, with the strongest documented gains where practice volume was previously the constraint. Anxiety reduction is a recurring finding — a bot is a low-stakes interlocutor.
- [Lyu et al. (2025), Effectiveness of chatbots in improving language learning: a meta-analysis, IJAL](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/ijal.12668); [AI-driven chatbots in L2 education: systematic review (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2215039025000086); [AI conversation bots, L2 speaking and speaking anxiety (Nature HSSC)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05550-z); [ChatGPT in language learning: systematic review (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590291125010885)

**Documented failure modes.**
1. **Level mismatch / too-perfect input.** Reviews flag doubts about chatbots' capacity to handle **low-proficiency learners**; prior systems "struggled with personalization and the consideration of language proficiency levels." An LLM's default register is native-fluent, which violates A6's coverage band on every turn unless explicitly constrained.
2. **Generic and repetitive feedback.** Bots produce "generic or repetitive feedback," and struggle with nuanced or culturally embedded language — degrading exactly the metalinguistic precision A4 depends on.
3. **Disengagement from irrelevant responses.** Learners in storytelling/discussion tasks disengaged when responses were generic or off-target.
4. **Over-reliance.** Consistently named across reviews — the bot does the composing.
5. **Limited emotional sensitivity**, hurting tasks needing empathy or interpretation — directly relevant to the A9 correction budget.
6. **Over-correction** is the predictable LLM pathology given A4+A9: the model can see every error and by default surfaces all of them. Reviews stress **personalized error correction** and adaptivity as the features that separate effective systems.
- [AI chatbots in L2 writing: anxiety, usefulness, outcome (Nature HSSC)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-026-06816-w) — notes avoidance behavior in high-anxiety writers and the need for adaptive designs across proficiency levels.

# PART B — Teaching linguistics as a discipline

## B1. The linguistic-problem tradition is a genuine pedagogy, not a novelty

**Claim.** The Linguistics Olympiad self-contained problem is a defined genre with explicit design criteria. Derzhanski & Payne's guidelines specify:

> (a) All problems must deal with real languages… (b) Everything in the problem must be accurate. Simplification is very rarely acceptable. **Made-up data is never allowed.** (c) Care must be taken to be respectful of the language and its speakers… (d) Problems must be **self-sufficient**: all the information needed to solve a problem must be present in the data or be common knowledge. Advanced linguistic terminology is avoided… (e) **Simple analogy cannot be the only strategy** needed to solve a problem. "Multi-stage logic" is required. (f) **Sheer insight should not be the only way** to solve a problem… (g) There can be **one and only one** reasonable solution, and that solution must be consistent with the facts of the language.

- [Payne & Derzhanski, Guidelines for Linguistic Problem Creation (PDF)](https://pages.uoregon.edu/tpayne/ProblemCreation.pdf)

Their worked contrast is the operational core. A **bad** Turkish problem:

```
deniz 'sea' / denizler 'seas' / denizde 'in the sea' / resim 'picture'
→ ___ 'pictures'  ___ 'in the picture'
```
Bad because the answers are "directly inferred… as though the subject language were a kind of coded English" — pure analogy, one step.

A **good** one adds a second stem with different suffix allomorphs (`okul` 'school', `okullar`, `okullara`) and asks for 'in the seas' — for which **no direct analogical model exists**. The solver must independently discover that plural precedes case, *and* that vowel harmony conditions the suffix shape, and compose them. Payne: "no knowledge of suffixation, plurality or case, much less vowel harmony, is necessary… these features are **discovered** by the solver."

**Why it works for linguistics specifically.** Payne notes solving is largely independent of prior educational background — "memorization of facts is useless" — and that surprising typological facts (Swahili's week starting Saturday; Hawaiian sibling terms encoding relative age *and* same-/opposite-sex; Samoan nouns where **singularity** is marked and plurality unmarked) "are revealed, not by instruction, but by discovery in the process of solving engaging logic puzzles." The genre is also methodologically honest: problems are contributed by working researchers "so that the phenomena represented in the competitions reflect the issues addressed and the concepts and methods used in contemporary professional linguistics."
- Training pedagogy, explicitly problem-based-learning framed: [Neacșu, *Linguistics Olympiad: Training Guide*, Language Science Press (open access PDF)](https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/handle/20.500.12657/93394/external_content.pdf) · [catalog page](https://langsci-press.org/catalog/book/420)

**Tutor implication.** Constraints (b), (d), (e), (g) are directly actionable and are exactly where an LLM tutor will fail without them. LLMs happily invent plausible data — which violates (b) fatally, since the learner's whole exercise is inferring a real system, and an invented one teaches a fiction. **Do not generate novel data in a language you cannot verify.** Use attested problems (IOL/NACLO archives, published data) or restrict generation to languages with well-documented paradigms and state the source. Then check the generated problem against (e) — if every answer is one-step analogy, it teaches nothing — and (g) — if two analyses fit the data, the problem is broken, not deep.

## B2. Discovery works — but only guided discovery

**Claim.** Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006) argue that minimally guided instruction fails for novices on cognitive-architecture grounds: novices lack the schemas that make unguided search tractable, so working memory is consumed by search rather than schema construction. Alfieri et al. (2011) quantified it: **unassisted discovery underperformed explicit instruction, d = −0.38**, and worse in math, science, problem solving, and verbal skills, especially for adolescents. But their *second* comparison found **enhanced/guided discovery — scaffolding, worked examples, or formative feedback — outperformed both** unguided discovery and pure explicit instruction.
- [Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006), Educational Psychologist 41(2):75–86](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1) · [full PDF](https://research.ou.nl/ws/files/1015152/Why%20minimal%20guidance%20during%20instruction%20does%20not%20work.pdf); [Alfieri et al. (2011) summary](https://laurenmarg.com/2017/08/13/article-summary-alfieri-et-al-2011-does-discovery-based-instruction-enhance-learning/); [Clark, Kirschner & Sweller, *American Educator* (2012) (PDF)](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/GuidedInstruction.pdf)

**The complication — Productive Failure.** Kapur's RCTs found that problem-solving-before-instruction produced **significantly better conceptual understanding and transfer** than instruction-first, **without sacrificing procedural fluency** — but the effect depends on *designed* struggle: the sequence always ends in explicit instruction, and the benefit came from scaffolding *how* students struggled. This is not a defense of unguided discovery; it is a claim about *ordering* within a guided design. Note it cuts against the **expertise reversal effect**, which would predict correct examples first.
- [Kapur (2014), Productive Failure in Learning Math, *Cognitive Science*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cogs.12107) · [PDF](https://www.cse.iitk.ac.in/users/se367/14/Readings/papers/kapur-14_productive-failure-in-learning-math.pdf); [Sinha & Kapur (2021), *Review of Educational Research*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543211019105); [Kapur & Roll, Productive Failure (PDF)](https://boldscience.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Productive-Failure.pdf)

**Tutor implication.** Puzzle-first is defensible and probably optimal for linguistics — **but only if the session reliably ends with explicit consolidation.** The failure mode is a tutor that lets a learner flounder and calls it discovery. Concretely: let the learner attempt, let them generate wrong analyses (that's the productive part), then *always* deliver the canonical formulation with terminology. Apply expertise reversal in reverse: as the learner gains competence, reduce scaffolding, not the struggle.

## B3. Argue from data — the actual skill

**Claim.** The transferable skill an intro linguistics course builds is not knowing IPA or X-bar; it is **constructing an argument from distributional evidence**. Standard phonology problem sets ask students to "posit **and justify** a phonemic analysis" — the justification is the assignment. The machinery: complementary distribution vs. contrast, minimal pairs as the contrast test, natural classes as the constraint on which generalizations are permissible, and the requirement that a rule state a *general* condition rather than list environments.
- [Assignment: phonology practice problems (Smith)](https://brianwilliamsmith.github.io/teaching/401_HW1.html); [MIT 24.900 problem sets](https://web.mit.edu/24.900/www/homework.html) · [worked phonology pset solution (PDF)](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/90956/24-900-spring-2005/contents/assignments/pset8_solution.pdf); [Zsiga, *The Sounds of Language*](https://www.amazon.com/Sounds-Language-Introduction-Phonetics-Phonology/dp/1405191031) — built around practicing data analysis and hypothesis testing
- Constructed-language pedagogy: students who *built* a phonology came to understand "how phonological rules work, and **why** rules surface in the first place," capturing generalizations over natural classes. [Phonology from the Inside Out, *Canadian Journal of Linguistics*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/canadian-journal-of-linguistics-revue-canadienne-de-linguistique/article/phonology-from-the-inside-out-constructed-language-as-a-pedagogical-tool/649F8BEDBF579512088F614EB2794B59)

**Tutor implication.** Grade the *argument*, not the answer. A correct analysis with no supporting distributional evidence should get pushback identical to a wrong one — otherwise you train answer-guessing, which is precisely what a fluent LLM tutor will reward by default. Demand: what is the generalization, what data supports it, **what datum would falsify it**, and is there a competing analysis you rejected and why. The falsification question is the one most tutors skip and the one that converts pattern-matching into linguistics.

## B4. Predictable student difficulties

1. **Prescriptive intuitions.** Students arrive equating "grammatical" with "correct," treating attested-but-stigmatized forms as errors in the data. Intro courses address this in the first weeks, but the intuition returns whenever the data is English. [Barber & Stainton, Linguistic Prescriptivism (PDF)](https://uwo.scholaris.ca/bitstreams/04a5054d-c9f1-4fc9-bdf2-f9597a186fe4/download)
2. **Transcription.** Documented error profile: **substitutions most common, then omissions; replacing IPA symbols with capital-letter lookalikes is the single most frequent error**; classic failure is transcribing *ink* as [ɪnk] rather than [ɪŋk] — a case where orthography overrides the student's own articulation. Orthographic interference runs both directions, and dictionary transcriptions don't consistently match the IPA chart, adding a further obstacle. [Phonetic-practice based training (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590291125005583); [EFL instructors' views on phonetic symbols (ERIC PDF)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1428944.pdf); [Impact of phonemic transcription on spelling](https://jlcc.spcrd.org/index.php/jlcc/article/view/152)
3. **Tree drawing.** Six recurring problems identified: determining which phrase structure rules apply, placing words and phrases, determining word class, handling long sentences, drawing movement arrows, and identifying structural ambiguity — plus **ternary branching** and mislabeled categories. Causes cited: insufficient procedural knowledge and **lack of confidence**. [Drawing Tree Diagrams: Problems and Suggestions (PDF)](https://www.academypublication.com/issues/past/jltr/vol01/06/29.pdf); [Syntactic Tree Diagram: Students' Problems and the Causes](https://journal.univetbantara.ac.id/index.php/ijelle/article/view/3717)
4. **English-intuition over-reliance.** Payne's design rationale addresses this directly: familiarity with the language in a problem "would sooner be an obstacle than an advantage." Students transfer English category assumptions onto unfamiliar data — expecting subject-initial order, expecting plurality to be the marked value, expecting tense where the language marks evidentiality (cf. his Quechua problem, where the contrast is direct knowledge / hearsay / conjecture, not tense).

**Tutor implication.** These are all *diagnosable from text*. Ternary branching, capital-letter IPA, and [ɪnk] are pattern-matchable errors with known underlying causes, so a tutor should name the cause, not just the fix ("you wrote what you spell, not what you say — say *ink* slowly and feel where your tongue is"). For #4, the highest-value intervention is deliberately choosing data where English intuitions mislead, then making the mismatch the lesson.

# Directly implementable rules

## A. Foreign-language tutoring

1. **Meaning-primary, form-reactive.** Every session is built on a task with a non-linguistic outcome. Attend to form when it blocks or degrades the message, then return to the task. Never open with a grammar topic. (A3, A10)
2. **Prompt before you supply.** On a learner error, escalate: clarification request → elicitation → metalinguistic cue → explicit correction. Give the correct form only after two prompts fail. Prompts outperform recasts for grammar. (A4)
3. **Recast only for phonology/spelling, for above-level forms, or to protect flow.** When you recast: ≤5 morphemes, ≤2 changes, one target, and **visually mark the change** — text has no intonation. (A4)
4. **One correction per turn.** Choose by: blocks comprehension > current teaching focus > repeated error. Let everything else pass silently; batch non-urgent items into an end-of-turn form note. (A4, A9)
5. **Correct immediately and inline.** In text-based CMC immediate feedback wins, and synchronous correction keeps meaning and form contiguous. (A4)
6. **Hit 95–98% known-word coverage in every text you generate.** ~2 unknown items per 50 words unaided, ~5 per 100 when glossing. Actively suppress your default native-fluent register; over-perfect input is the LLM tutor's signature failure. (A6, A11)
7. **Store chunks with their frames, never bare word pairs.** Formulaic sequences are 50–80% of real language and are what fluency is made of. (A7)
8. **Engineer 8–10 encounters per item, each in a different context, via narrow input.** Keep a topic or author thread running across sessions rather than a fresh topic each time. (A7)
9. **Interleave, never block.** Mix the 2–3 structures the learner actually confuses in a single session, space across days, and tell the learner it will feel harder and work better. (A8)
10. **Test by production, never recognition.** Multiple choice measures nothing the learner can do in conversation. (A8)
11. **Run dictogloss and short L1→L2 translation regularly.** Reconstruct, then compare clause by clause against the original — the comparison step is what generates language-related episodes. (A5)
12. **Log every language-related episode as the review queue.** Forms the learner consciously puzzled over are the live acquisition frontier; forms you corrected unilaterally are not. (A5)
13. **Repeat tasks.** Same task cold, then again after form feedback. Rehearsal is the cheapest accuracy lever available in text. Announce which of complexity / accuracy / fluency each task is optimizing — you cannot have all three. (A10)
14. **Treat WTC as the resource being spent.** If output volume drops after a correction-heavy stretch, cut the correction budget immediately. Anxiety is self-exacerbating and correction harshness feeds it. Frame errors as data. (A9)
15. **Never do the composing.** Over-reliance is the most consistently reported chatbot failure — prompt the learner toward the form; don't hand them the sentence. (A11)

## B. Linguistics tutoring

1. **Puzzle first, instruction always.** Give data before terminology, let the learner attempt and fail productively, then **always** close with the canonical formulation and the technical vocabulary. Unguided discovery is d = −0.38; guided discovery beats both alternatives. Never end a session on unresolved struggle. (B2)
2. **Never invent language data.** Use attested problems (IOL/NACLO archives, published grammars, documented paradigms). Made-up data is disqualifying in the genre and teaches a fiction. Cite the source; flag any simplification. (B1)
3. **Enforce the multi-stage-logic test.** If every answer follows by direct analogy from a given item, rebuild the problem — add a second stem, an allomorph, or a target with no analogical model (Payne's `okul`/`deniz` contrast). (B1)
4. **Enforce unique solvability.** One reasonable analysis consistent with the facts. Two competing analyses that fit equally means the data set is underdetermined — add data rather than declaring the ambiguity profound. (B1)
5. **Grade the argument, not the answer.** A right answer with no distributional justification gets the same pushback as a wrong one. Ask: what's the generalization, what data supports it, **what would falsify it**, what competing analysis did you reject and why. (B3)
6. **Require natural classes, not lists.** A rule stated as an enumeration of environments is a description; a rule stated over a natural class is an analysis. Push until it generalizes. (B3)
7. **Scaffold by ordering data, not by hinting.** When the learner stalls, re-present a subset that isolates one variable (all forms of one stem; the minimal pair that decides contrast vs. complementary distribution). Adding data preserves the discovery; giving the rule destroys it. (B1, B2)
8. **Choose data where English intuitions fail** — evidentiality instead of tense, unmarked plurals, ergative alignment, non-SVO order — and make the mismatch explicit once solved. Familiarity with the language is an obstacle, not an advantage. (B4)
9. **Separate "grammatical" from "correct" on first contact and re-separate it whenever the data is English.** Prescriptive intuition is the most persistent contaminant in intro syntax. (B4)
10. **Diagnose transcription errors by cause, not symptom.** Capital-letter IPA substitutions, and spelling-driven errors like [ɪnk] for [ɪŋk], are orthographic interference — say so and route the learner to articulation, not to the symbol chart. (B4)
11. **Check trees for the six known failure points**: phrase-structure rule choice, constituent placement, word-class assignment, long-sentence handling, movement arrows, and structural ambiguity — plus ternary branching. Confidence is a documented cause; sequence from short unambiguous sentences upward. (B4)
12. **Fade scaffolding as competence grows, not struggle.** Expertise reversal means the supports that helped at week one become noise by week ten; the difficulty of the data should rise while the guidance drops. (B2)

**Note on a genuine unresolved tension:** A3 (Norris & Ortega: explicit ≈ FonFS ≈ FonF, all large) and A4 (Lyster & Saito: prompts > recasts, especially on free response) partly disagree, and the disagreement tracks the outcome measure. Rules A1–A4 above resolve it in favor of the free-production evidence, which is the right call for a conversational tutor but is a design choice, not a settled finding. If your success metric were a discrete-point grammar test, the evidence would point toward more FonFS than these rules prescribe.
