# Curriculum Sequencing for an Individual Learner — Research Review

Scope: the science of ordering concepts for one learner. Written to evaluate Seba's syllabus model (a DAG of concepts, drafted once from a book's TOC, traversed one concept per session, frontier-first, binary done status).

Confidence tags used throughout: **[P]** = read the primary source; **[S]** = secondary/abstract only, not verified against the primary; **[W]** = weak or contested evidence base.

---

## 1. Learning progressions / learning trajectories

### 1.1 What they are

Clements & Sarama's canonical definition, quoted in the CPRE report:

> "descriptions of children's thinking and learning in a specific mathematical domain, and a related conjectured route through a set of instructional tasks designed to engender those mental processes or actions hypothesized to move children through a developmental progression of levels of thinking, created with the intent of supporting children's achievement of specific goals in that mathematical domain." (Clements & Sarama 2004, p. 83) **[P]**

Source: Daro, Mosher & Corcoran (2011), *Learning Trajectories in Mathematics: A Foundation for Standards, Curriculum, Assessment, and Instruction*, CPRE RR-68 — https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED519792.pdf (also https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED519792)

The critical structural point: a trajectory is **two coupled orderings**, not one. CPRE makes this explicit **[P]**:

> "there actually are two or more closely related and interacting trajectories or ordered paths aimed at reaching the goal(s): • Teachers use an ordered set of instructional experiences and tasks that are hypothesized to 'engender the mental processes or actions' … and • Students' 'thinking and learning… in a specific mathematical domain' go through a 'developmental progression of levels'"

The teaching order is a **hypothesis** about the learning order. It is not the learning order. CPRE: "The 'conjectured' or hypothesized order of experiences … will need to be checked against actual evidence of progress, presumably to be revised and retried if the hypotheses prove false or faulty." **[P]**

### 1.2 Are they linear or lattice-like? — Lattice-like, and explicitly *not* a stage ladder

Confrey's OECD commissioned paper is the clearest statement **[P]**:

> "most LT theorists (Lehrer & Schauble, 2015; Maloney, Confrey & Nguyen, 2014; Battista, 2011) **reject a strict stage theory** as a model for LT/LPs. LT/LP theorists recognise that the levels are **not rigidly sequenced** (NRC, 2007, p. 221), and that **students advance and fall back**, making steady progress when viewed over time … These theorists regard the ordered sequence of levels as **'expected probabilities'**"

> "LT/LPs can be likened to ladders, with students ascending a rung at a time, but this image can inadvertently reinforce the stage theory model. Battista (2011) refers to the levels as a variety of plateaus of different heights. To capture the flexibility in students' paths, Confrey & Toutkoushian (in press) use the metaphor of a **climbing wall**, which represents a conceptual space that presents both handholds and obstacles, and **supports multiple starting points and routes**."

And the shared commitment among all LT theorists, quoting NRC 2007 p. 221:

> "**all students will follow not one general sequence, but multiple (often interacting) sequences**"

Source: Confrey, J. (2018), *A Synthesis of Research on Learning Trajectories/Progressions in Mathematics*, OECD EDU/EDPC(2018)44/ANN3 — https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/about/projects/edu/education-2040/publications/commissioned-papers/Confrey,%20J.pdf

CPRE independently confirms non-monotonicity **[P]**:

> "Students' performances with respect to learning trajectories … **do not simply increase monotonically. Rather students move back and forth along the trajectory** as they interact with new contexts or more complex [problems]"

and on individual variation:

> "The complexity of the manifold of learning trajectories guarantees that teachers will encounter **a wide variety of individual mathematical biographies**"

Confrey's own deployed system, Math-Mapper 6-8, is structurally a **DAG-of-DAGs**, not a chain: "a map of nine big ideas organised hierarchically into 23 clusters made from 62 learning trajectories" **[P]**. So the field's own operational artifact *is* a graph — which supports the DAG shape while denying that traversal is a single linear walk.

### 1.3 How are validated progressions actually built?

Not from a table of contents. The pipeline, per Confrey **[P]**:

1. Design studies / teaching experiments over extended periods, observing student thinking.
2. Articulate levels tied to *theoretical mechanisms* for level transitions.
3. Develop tasks mapped to the levels.
4. Fit measurement models (IRT / Wright maps / construct maps à la Wilson).
5. **Empirical recovery**: check whether the hypothesized order actually reproduces in item-difficulty data.
6. Revise.

Concretely, Confrey, Toutkoushian & Shah (2019): from 37,000 assessments over three years, **167 of 676 calibrated items (24%) were identified as non-conforming and revised** — items were removed, re-levelled, re-sequenced, or the LT was rebuilt **[P]**. Confrey's conclusion: validation "is an ongoing process," not a one-time act.

Validation cycle framework: Graf, E. A. & van Rijn, P. W. (2016), reproduced in Confrey's OECD paper; see also Alonzo & Elby (2019), *A cycle for validating a learning progression* — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0732312320301000 **[S]**

### 1.4 Do they work?

Yes, when built this way. Clements & Sarama's Building Blocks / TRIAD is the strongest evidence in this literature: cluster-randomized trial, 1,375 preschoolers, 106 classrooms, 42 schools; **d = 0.47 vs. an active comparison curriculum, d = 1.07 vs. business-as-usual control**; large-scale replication **d = 0.72**, with effects persisting to grade 5 for some subgroups **[S]**.
- https://www.researchgate.net/publication/258933028_Mathematics_Learned_by_Young_Children_in_an_Intervention_Based_on_Learning_Trajectories_A_Large-Scale_Cluster_Randomized_Trial
- https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/scaling-triad-teaching-early-mathematics-understanding-trajectories-and-technologies
- Clements, Sarama, Baroody, Joswick & Wolfe (2019), shape-composition LT efficacy — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/0002831219842788

Important confound: TRIAD bundles the trajectory *with* a curriculum, teacher PD, and formative assessment. The RCT does not isolate "correct sequence" as the active ingredient. CPRE is candid that the significance of trajectory-based design "depends on the rigor of the empirical work that supports the hypothetical trajectories" **[P]**.

### 1.5 The generalization limit that matters most for Seba

CPRE, on the "hierarchic interaction" hypothesis **[P]**:

> "the influence of more universal and internal factors relative to variations in external experience and instruction would become **less and less as students get older and the mathematics becomes more advanced**, and that the **range of variation due to differences in experience will certainly increase**."

Validated progressions are overwhelmingly K–8, in a handful of domains (number, fractions, measurement, early geometry, early algebra, a few science constructs). For an adult self-directed learner working through, say, measure theory or Italian subjunctive usage, **no validated progression exists**, and the theory itself predicts that inter-learner variation is at its largest exactly there. Anything Seba drafts is a *hypothetical* learning trajectory in Simon's (1995) original sense — and Simon's HLT was designed to be revised after every lesson.

## 2. Whole-task sequencing vs. prerequisite-first traversal — the central conflict

### 2.1 The prerequisite-first tradition

**Gagné's learning hierarchies.** Task analysis proceeds top-down by repeatedly asking "what must the learner already be able to do?" until you hit entry behaviours; instruction then proceeds bottom-up. Gagné, *The Conditions of Learning*; hierarchy chapter — http://www.sietmanagement.fr/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/Gagne%CC%81.pdf. This is precisely Seba's model: a DAG with prereq edges, traversed frontier-first.

Empirical status: White (1974), *The Validation of a Learning Hierarchy*, AERJ — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00028312011002121 **[S]**. Support is strongest for *generalized intellectual skills* (procedures) and weakest-to-absent for *verbalized knowledge* (facts/concepts) **[S]**. This is a material caveat: much of what a concept-DAG tutor teaches (definitions, conceptual relations, vocabulary) sits on the side where hierarchy theory does *not* find support.

**Knowledge Space Theory** is the mathematically serious descendant, and the strongest evidence that prerequisite structures are real and usable. Doignon & Falmagne (1985); *Knowledge Spaces and Learning Spaces* — https://arxiv.org/pdf/1511.06757. A knowledge structure is a family of feasible knowledge *states* closed under union (a knowledge space) and, with well-gradedness, a learning space. The **outer fringe** of a state — items q such that K ∪ {q} is also a feasible state — is exactly "the set of items the student is ready to learn." **[P]**

ALEKS operationalizes this at scale. Cosyn, Uzun, Doble & Matayoshi (2021), *A practical perspective on knowledge space theory: ALEKS and its data*, J. Math. Psych. — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022249621000134. Key figures **[P]**:
- ~4–5 million students/year; several hundred items per course; ~1,100 items available for Algebra I.
- Learning-attempt **success rate on outer-fringe items: median 0.863** across 500 most-used Algebra I items; 92.4% of items ≥ 0.70; only 14/500 below 0.60.
- The authors explicitly read low success rate as a **diagnostic that the knowledge structure is wrong** for that item: "the item may not belong in the outer fringe of the knowledge states from which the learning is attempted."

Three things Seba should absorb from ALEKS: (a) the frontier concept is empirically validated and works; (b) the structure is over **states**, not a single path — many orderings are legal and the learner picks among them; (c) **the structure is instrumented and corrected from data**, continuously.

Automated prerequisite-structure discovery is an active area — Annabi & Nguyen (2023), *Prerequisite Structure Discovery in Intelligent Tutoring Systems*, IEEE ICDL — https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01672 — which is itself evidence that hand-authored prerequisite graphs are known to be unreliable enough to be worth learning from data.

### 2.2 The whole-task tradition

**Elaboration Theory (Reigeluth).** The "zoom lens": start with an **epitome** — not a summary or abstract, but a *simplified whole version* of the task/theory that applies the same ideas at their simplest real level — then elaborate by zooming in, returning periodically to the wide-angle view to synthesize.
- Reigeluth & Stein (1983), *The Elaboration Theory of Instruction* — https://ocw.metu.edu.tr/pluginfile.php/9337/mod_resource/content/1/Reigeluth%201983%20Article.pdf
- Reigeluth (1992), *Elaborating the elaboration theory*, ETR&D — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/225122797_Elaborating_the_elaboration_theory_51
- https://www.instructionaldesign.org/theories/elaboration-theory/

The key claim, and the one that directly contradicts DAG traversal: **the first lesson should epitomize rather than summarize** — the learner does a real, complete, simplest-case version of the target task *before* the components are taught. A prerequisite-first DAG guarantees the opposite: the learner meets N component concepts before ever seeing the thing they compose into.

Empirical status: **weak [W]**. Reigeluth's own literature concedes "there has been relatively little empirical research on the theory." The main test is *formative research*, not a comparison trial: English & Reigeluth (1996), *Formative research on sequencing instruction with the elaboration theory*, ETR&D 44(1), 23–42 — four chapters of an electrical-circuit-analysis text revised per the theory, **n = 13 college sophomores**, two phases, interactive then non-interactive data collection **[S]**. That is a design-improvement study, not evidence that elaboration sequences beat alternatives.

**Merrill's First Principles / Component Display Theory.** The synthesis principle: "instruction should use a **progression of increasingly complex whole tasks**"; components are taught *in the context of* whole problems rather than separately.
- Merrill (2002), *First Principles of Instruction* — https://mdavidmerrill.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/firstprinciplesreigeluthcarr-1.pdf
- Merrill (2007), *A Task-Centered Instructional Strategy* — https://app.nova.edu/toolbox/instructionalproducts/edd8124/articles/2007-Merrill-A%20task-centered_Instructional_Strategy.pdf
First Principles is a *synthesis* of other theories, not an independently tested one **[W]**.

**4C/ID (van Merriënboer).** The most operationally specific of the three. Learning tasks are whole-task instantiations forming the curriculum backbone, grouped into **task classes** of increasing complexity, with **decreasing scaffolding within a class** and **high variability of practice across variants within a class**. Part-task practice exists but is subordinate — used only for routine constituent skills that need automation, and always *alongside* whole-task work, never before it.
- van Merriënboer, Clark & de Croock (2002), *Blueprints for complex learning: The 4C/ID model*, ETR&D — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/225798787_Blueprints_for_complex_learning_The_4CID-model
- https://edutechwiki.unige.ch/en/4C-ID
- Recent application: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12700520/

Reported evidence favours whole-task on both skill-acquisition and transfer tests **[S]**; 4C/ID has the broadest applied uptake (medical, vocational, technical training) of the three.

### 2.3 Adjudicating the conflict

These are **not** simply contradictory; they answer different questions, and the honest reading is:

| | Prerequisite DAG (Gagné/KST) | Whole-task (ET/4C/ID/Merrill) |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of sequencing | knowledge component / item | whole task / problem class |
| Best-supported domain | procedural skills, well-structured math | complex integrated skills, transfer goals |
| Evidence quality | strong (KST/ALEKS at scale; White 1974) | moderate for 4C/ID **[S]**, thin for ET **[W]** |
| Motivational profile | components are meaningless until late | purpose visible from lesson 1 |

The real contradiction is narrower than it looks: **both traditions agree that a component should not be taught before its prerequisites are available.** They disagree about whether the *organizing spine* of the curriculum is the component graph or the task progression. 4C/ID's answer — task classes as the spine, with just-in-time information and part-task practice hanging off them — is compatible with a prereq DAG used as a *constraint checker* rather than as an *agenda*.

For Seba specifically: the DAG can stay, but topologically sorting it and walking the frontier makes the DAG the agenda. That is the choice the whole-task literature objects to, and it is also the choice that produces the worst motivational profile for a self-directed adult learner who has a goal in mind.

## 3. Spiral curriculum

Bruner (1960): topics are revisited, each revisit at greater complexity, each connected to prior learning. Harden & Stamper (1999), *What is a spiral curriculum?*, Med Teacher — https://med.virginia.edu/faculty-affairs/wp-content/uploads/sites/458/2016/04/2010-3-23.pdf

Evidence: **thin and largely non-experimental [W]**. Woodward (2019), *The Spiral Curriculum in Higher Education*, e-JBEST 13(3) — https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1258243.pdf — is a small qualitative document/interview analysis of one business studies programme; on the evidence gathered, its own conclusion is that the asserted typology did "not at all seem to be bringing" the claimed structure through **[P]**. The commonly cited criticism (Coelho & Moles 2015, discussed in Woodward **[P]**) is a **learner-facing** one and is directly relevant to Seba: *students cannot tell where their learning should stop or be deemed sufficient at a given stage without knowing the revisiting point.* A spiral without a published revisit schedule reads to the learner as failure to finish.

The mechanism that *is* well evidenced underneath "spiral" is spacing (§6). "Spiral curriculum" as normally implemented is spacing plus depth escalation, and the spacing part carries the effect. Treat Bruner as a design frame, not as evidence.

**Interaction with a DAG whose nodes are "done":** direct conflict. `done` is a terminal absorbing state; a spiral has no terminal state. Note that the LT literature independently rejects the terminal state for a different reason — students "move back and forth along the trajectory" (CPRE **[P]**) and levels represent "expected probabilities" (Confrey **[P]**). Two independent literatures converge on: **a concept's status should be a decaying scalar, not a latch.**

## 4. Backward design

Wiggins & McTighe, *Understanding by Design*: three stages — (1) identify desired results, (2) determine acceptable evidence (design the assessment *before* the instruction), (3) plan learning experiences.
- https://educationaltechnology.net/wp-content/uploads/2016/01/backward-design.pdf
- ASCD UbD white paper — https://files.ascd.org/staticfiles/ascd/pdf/siteASCD/publications/UbD_WhitePaper0312.pdf
- McTighe's own research compilation — https://jaymctighe.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/UbD-Research-Studies.docx-11.25.25.pdf **[S]**
- Cho & Trent (2005), critical treatment — https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ795704.pdf

Evidence: positive but implementation-dependent, and the compiled evidence base is largely author-curated and quasi-experimental **[W]**. The claim "UbD enhances cognitive engagement and achievement… however, successful implementation depends upon adequate teacher training and ongoing systemic support" is a hedge that should be read as: the framework is a planning discipline, not an intervention with an effect size.

The *design discipline* is nonetheless directly applicable and cheap: **"what performance would count as evidence the learner reached the goal?" should be answered before the concept list is drafted.** Seba currently derives its syllabus from a book's TOC, which is stage-3-first: it inherits an author's coverage decisions, chapter-length conventions, and ordering-for-exposition (which is optimized for a reader who can flip back, not for a sequential tutee). A TOC is a *scope* artifact; it is not a *goal* artifact. Backward design says the goal should filter the TOC, not the reverse.

## 5. Does concept ORDER actually matter? — the empirical question

This is where the review should make Seba nervous, and where the evidence is genuinely mixed.

**What is well established:** order effects exist and can be large in *category learning*, *problem-solving strategy discrimination*, and *practice scheduling*. Ritter, Nerb, Lehtinen & O'Shea (eds.), *In Order to Learn: How the Sequence of Topics Influences Learning*, Oxford — https://academic.oup.com/book/10528 **[S]** — the whole volume exists because sequence manipulations reliably move outcomes at the *item/practice* level.

**What is not well established: that reordering a valid prerequisite structure at the topic level changes outcomes.** The recurring finding in the elaboration/hierarchy literature is null. The claim that circulates in reviews of hierarchical sequencing is that experiments with high school students found **no differences between the empirically derived sequence and reordered inter- or intra-sequences on time to mastery, nor in number of skills mastered** **[S — I could not verify this against the primary source; it appears in secondary summaries of the elaboration-theory literature, and should be traced to Wilson & Cole (1992) / Reigeluth's hierarchy reviews before being relied on]**.

**The best-controlled modern evidence is a simulation, and it says "sometimes":** *Simulation-Based Evaluation of Learning Sequences for Instructional Technologies*, Instructional Science 45 (2017) — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-016-9369-x **[S]** — finds that **different valid instructional sequences for the same hierarchy produce significantly different simulated outcomes**, and, importantly, that **student ability interacts with sequence and hierarchy structure to disadvantage lower-ability learners**. Read carefully, this says the *topology* constrains a set of legal orders, and choice *within* that set matters more for weaker learners than stronger ones.

**Convergent evidence from the KC literature:** Koedinger, Carvalho, Liu & McLaughlin (2023), *An Astonishing Regularity in Student Learning Rate*, PNAS — https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2221311120 (preprint text read **[P]**). Across 1.3M observations / 27 datasets: initial pre-practice accuracy ~65% and **varies a lot** across students (55% lower half vs 75% upper half), but **learning rate is astonishingly uniform** (~0.1 log-odds, ~2.5% accuracy per opportunity), and a typical student needs **~7 practice opportunities per knowledge component** to reach 80%.

The implication for sequencing is sharp and underappreciated: if learners differ mainly in *where they start* and barely at all in *how fast they climb*, then **the highest-leverage personalization is not reordering the sequence — it is correctly locating the learner's entry point and giving each component enough opportunities.** Sequence optimization is a second-order lever; entry-point diagnosis and practice sufficiency are first-order.

**Honest summary:** order matters, but (a) most of the demonstrated effect lives at the practice-scheduling level, not the topic level; (b) among *prerequisite-legal* topic orders, effects are small-to-null in the studies that looked; (c) the payoff from getting order right is concentrated in weaker/lower-prior-knowledge learners. **Getting the DAG "right" is worth much less than Seba's design implies. Getting the starting point and the amount of practice right is worth much more.**

## 6. Interleaving and spacing at the syllabus level

This is the strongest evidence in this entire review, and it is an argument against contiguous single-concept units.

**Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung (2020), *A Randomized Controlled Trial of Interleaved Mathematics Practice*, JEP** — https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2019-rohrer.pdf **[P]**. Preregistered, cluster-randomized, **54 seventh-grade classes**, 4 months, naturalistic, teachers untrained. Both groups saw the **identical problems — only the order differed.** Unannounced test one month later: **interleaved 61% vs blocked 38%, d = 0.83.**

Read that again in Seba's terms: **same content, same total time, order-only manipulation, d = 0.83, one-month-delayed test.** This is the single largest sequencing effect in the review, and it is an effect *against* the blocked, one-topic-at-a-time arrangement Seba implements.

Supporting facts from the same paper **[P]**: an audit of six 7th-grade textbooks found **78% of practice problems blocked, 11% interleaved** (Dedrick, Rohrer & Stershic 2016) — i.e., the TOC-derived default is the arrangement that loses. And the structural point: "interleaved practice guarantees spaced practice, because when the practice of multiple skills is interleaved (ABCBACBCA) rather than blocked (AAABBBCCC), the practice of any one skill is necessarily spaced."

Related: Rohrer & Hartwig, *Spaced and Interleaved Mathematics Practice* — https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-spaced-and-interleaved-mathematics-practice-rohrer-hartwig.pdf; Rohrer, Dedrick & Hartwig (2020), *Unanswered questions about spaced interleaved mathematics practice* — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S221136812030053X

Caveat worth stating: the interleaving benefit is best demonstrated for **discriminable, confusable skills within a domain** (which strategy applies?). It is *not* established that interleaving unrelated topics helps. So the recommendation is not "shuffle the syllabus" — it is "once a set of related concepts is in-progress, practice across them rather than one at a time."

Spacing at the review level: Cepeda et al. (2008), *Spacing effects in learning: a temporal ridgeline of optimal retention*, Psych Science — the optimal gap scales with the retention interval, roughly 10–20%. https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2008-cepeda.pdf **[P, abstract]**

## 7. Concept granularity — how big should a unit be?

**Segmenting effect.** Rey, Beege, Nebel, Wirzberger, Schmitt & Schneider (2019), *A Meta-analysis of the Segmenting Effect*, Educ Psych Rev — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4 **[P, full text]**. 56 investigations, 88 pairwise comparisons, N up to 7,713. Results:
- Retention **d = 0.32** (SE .06, p < .001); Transfer **d = 0.36** (SE .06, p < .001)
- Overall cognitive load reduced, **d = 0.23**
- **Learning time increased, d = −0.92** — segmenting costs real time
- **System-paced** segmentation: retention d = 0.42, transfer d = 0.35 — *both significant*
- **Learner-paced** segmentation: retention **d = 0.19, p = 0.10 — not significant**; transfer d = 0.45
- Moderator: **high prior knowledge learners benefit *more* from segmenting on retention** (counter-intuitive; usually expertise-reversal runs the other way)
- Massive heterogeneity throughout (Q = 2093.94, df = 66) — the mean effect is weakly interpretable

Two implications: segmenting into coherent units is mildly beneficial and costs time; **system-paced segmentation outperformed learner-paced**, which is a small point in favour of Seba choosing the unit rather than the learner.

**How much practice per unit?** Koedinger et al. (2023) **[P]**: **~7 practice opportunities per knowledge component** to reach 80% accuracy, with substantial variation across students in *opportunities needed* driven almost entirely by initial performance, not rate. This is the best available number for calibrating "how big is a concept."

**Mastery learning unit sizing (Keller Plan / PSI).** Keller (1968), "Good-bye, teacher…", JABA. PSI = small, self-paced, modularized units with a mastery gate before advancing. Kulik, Kulik & Cohen (1979), 75 studies, ~8 percentage points exam gain; Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990), *Effectiveness of Mastery Learning Programs: A Meta-Analysis*, RER, 108 controlled evaluations, positive effects, **stronger for weaker students** — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543060002265 **[S]**. Modern systematic review: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10864-023-09530-8 **[S]**. PSI is the closest historical analogue to Seba's design and it *worked* — but note that PSI's units were sized to a mastery *test*, and advancement was gated by a demonstrated criterion, not tutor judgement.

**Microlearning — be skeptical.** The recent meta-analysis reporting SMD = 0.74 for learning outcomes and OR = 1.87 for retention appears in *MATHEMA: Jurnal Pendidikan Matematika* — https://publikasi.teknokrat.ac.id/index.php/jurnalmathema/article/view/517 — a low-tier venue, with I² = 67–71% heterogeneity and no credible publication-bias handling **[W — I would not cite this to justify a design decision]**. The more careful review, Leong et al. (2021)/Sozmen et al. (2024), *Microlearning beyond boundaries*, Heliyon — https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11774797/ — concedes results are "scattered and mixed" **[S]**. There is **no defensible evidence base for a specific microlearning unit size.** Do not let "microlearning" justify est_sessions = 1.

## 8. Curriculum revision: is a fixed up-front plan defensible?

### 8.1 Curriculum scripts

Putnam, R. T. (1987), *Structuring and adjusting content for students: A study of live and simulated tutoring of addition*, American Educational Research Journal 24(1), 13–48 **[S — I was unable to retrieve the primary text; treat the following as the field's standard reading of it]**.

The standard account: Putnam had expert teachers tutor a simulated student with known bugs. Rather than diagnosing the student's specific misconception and repairing it, tutors ran a **"curriculum script"** — a pre-stored, well-rehearsed sequence of explanations, examples, and questions for the topic — moving through it and making local adjustments, but not building a model of *this* student's bug. The finding is genuinely double-edged and both edges matter for Seba:

- **Against fixed plans:** the experts were *worse* at individualized diagnosis than the ITS literature assumed; script-following substituted for model-building.
- **For fixed plans:** the experts were nonetheless *effective*, and the script is what made them fluent. A script is not a defect; it is compiled expertise.

Leinhardt & Greeno (1986), *The cognitive skill of teaching*, JEP: experts hold **agendas** (a lesson plan as a set of goals with expected action sequences) and are "constantly engaged in planning on different levels, based on tutoring schemas acquired through years of experience" **[S]** — cited in https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40593-015-0053-0 and http://users.sussex.ac.uk/~bend/papers/final%20modelling.pdf

### 8.2 Expert vs. novice plan deviation

The consistent finding: experts hold a plan **and** deviate opportunistically; novices either follow rigidly or abandon it. From the tutoring-modelling literature **[S]**:
- Two levels: **high-level policies** structuring the session, and **lower-level strategies** chosen *during* the session for local subgoals — and experts *change policy* in response to their assessment of student performance (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/255587927).
- "Experts work opportunistically, and so it may not be possible for the system to comment sensibly on an individual step until sufficient context for that step has been developed."
- Plan-based *opportunistic* architectures (Derry, Hawkes & Ziegler 1988) exist precisely to model this.

Baker, R. S. (2016), *Stupid Tutoring Systems, Intelligent Humans*, IJAIED — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40593-016-0105-0 — the systems that scale are the *simple* ones; the elaborate models did not carry the effect **[P, abstract]**. A useful corrective against replanning machinery for its own sake.

### 8.3 "Wheel spinning" — the failure mode a fixed plan cannot see

Beck & Gong's construct: a student **fails to master a KC despite substantial practice**. Zhang et al. (2019), *Early Detection of Wheel Spinning*, EDM — https://educationaldatamining.org/EDM2019/proceedings/ **[P]**: wheel spinning is detectable **early**, even by a logistic regression on a single feature, and "ideally, an ITS would detect this phenomenon early, so that the system or a teacher could try **alternative instructional strategies**."

This is the strongest single argument against Seba's fixed plan + `est_sessions: 1-3` + tutor-judged `done`: the modelled failure mode is *the learner is stuck and the plan has no branch for it*. A capped estimate with a binary terminal status will resolve stuck-ness by declaring `done`.

### 8.4 Verdict on fixed plans

The evidence supports a **script with branches**, not a plan and not improvisation:
- The *existence* of a pre-drafted plan is well supported (curriculum scripts, agendas, PSI modules, ALEKS knowledge structures, 4C/ID blueprints).
- **Never revising it is not supported by anything.** Confrey's own validated LTs get revised (24% of items). Simon's HLT is revised after every lesson by construction. ALEKS re-assesses on a schedule and recomputes the fringe. Expert tutors change policy mid-session. The one thing every source agrees on is that the plan is a hypothesis under test.

## 9. Who chooses the next topic?

### 9.1 The direct evidence: learner control does approximately nothing

**Karich, Burns & Maki (2014), *Updated Meta-Analysis of Learner Control Within Educational Technology*, Review of Educational Research 84(3)** — https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1034262 **[S]**. 85 articles screened, 18 met inclusion, 29 effects. **Overall g = 0.05** — "almost zero" — and "also near zero when examining most characteristics of control and classroom contextual factors." This updates Niemiec, Sikorski & Walberg, which found similarly little.

Corroborating from a different literature: in the segmenting meta-analysis **[P]**, **system-paced** segmentation produced significant retention gains (d = 0.42) while **learner-paced** did not (d = 0.19, p = .10).

So: giving the learner control over sequencing is not, on average, harmful — but there is no achievement dividend to buy with it.

### 9.2 Why: learners misjudge their own learning

Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell (2013), *Self-Regulated Learning: Beliefs, Techniques, and Illusions*, Annual Review of Psychology 64 — https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/pdf/10.1146/annurev-psych-113011-143823 (and gwern mirror https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2013-bjork.pdf) **[S]**. "People often have a faulty mental model of how they learn and remember, making them prone to both **misassessing and mismanaging** their own learning."

The canonical demonstration: learners **learn better from interleaved schedules but believe blocked was better** — the majority of Kornell & Bjork's participants got it backwards, and the *more effortful* they found interleaving, the *less* effective they judged it and the less likely they were to choose it again. Kornell & Bjork (2007), *The promise and perils of self-regulated study*, PB&R — https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03194055; https://web.williams.edu/Psychology/Faculty/Kornell/Publications/Kornell.Bjork.2007.pdf **[S]**. Also: allowing learners to **drop** items from a flashcard set had "small but consistently negative effects on learning" **[S]**.

Note the exact shape of the failure: **learners systematically prefer blocked over interleaved.** That is precisely the choice Seba's design already makes *for* them. Handing sequencing to the learner would not fix it; it would entrench it.

### 9.3 The counter-consideration: autonomy is not about achievement

ALEKS is instructive here **[P]**: the *system* computes the outer fringe (what is legal to learn next), and the **learner picks from it** — "The student chooses an item from her outer fringe." Constrained choice: the system owns correctness, the learner owns selection within the feasible set. Given g = 0.05, this costs nothing in achievement and buys engagement — which for a voluntary, self-directed adult learner is the binding constraint on whether sessions happen at all.

## 10. Verdicts on Seba's design

### (a) DAG of concepts with prereq edges — **SUPPORTED (with a structural caveat)**

Strongest support in the review. Knowledge Space Theory is the mature formalization (Doignon & Falmagne; https://arxiv.org/pdf/1511.06757) and ALEKS is its validation at 4–5M students/year with **median 0.863 success rate on outer-fringe items** — meaning prerequisite structures really do predict readiness. Gagné's hierarchies have direct validation for procedural skills (White 1974). Confrey's own deployed Math-Mapper is a graph (9 big ideas → 23 clusters → 62 LTs).

Caveats: (i) hierarchy validation is strong for *skills*, weak-to-absent for *verbalized knowledge* — much of what Seba teaches sits on the weak side; (ii) KST's structure is over **states**, admitting many legal orders, which is *more* permissive than a topological sort of a fixed DAG; (iii) hand-authored prerequisite graphs are unreliable enough that automated discovery is an active research area.

### (b) Syllabus drafted once from a book's TOC, never revised — **CONTRADICTED**

Two separate failures.

*Never revised* is contradicted by every source that has ever built a real progression. Simon's HLT is revised lesson-to-lesson by construction. Confrey's validated LTs had **24% of items (167/676) revised** after empirical recovery, and she frames validation as "an ongoing process." CPRE: the conjectured order "will need to be checked against actual evidence of progress, presumably to be **revised and retried** if the hypotheses prove false or faulty." ALEKS re-assesses and recomputes the fringe on a schedule; low success rates are read as *structure defects*. Expert tutors change policy mid-session. Wheel-spinning is detectable early and is defined by needing an alternative strategy — which a non-revisable plan cannot supply.

*From a TOC* is contradicted by backward design (start from target performance, not the source's coverage) and by the LT literature's core distinction: trajectories "differ fundamentally from **solely logical deconstructions** of the mathematical ideas" (Confrey) — a TOC is exactly a logical deconstruction, further distorted by an author's chapter-length conventions and by the assumption of a reader who can flip back. And empirically: textbook-derived ordering is the *blocked* arrangement that loses to interleaving by d = 0.83 (Rohrer et al. 2020).

The one thing a TOC *is* good for is scope coverage — it is a reasonable **seed**, and a bad **contract**.

### (c) One concept per session — **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED / arguably CONTRADICTED**

Support: the segmenting effect is real (retention d = 0.32, transfer d = 0.36; Rey et al. 2019), **system-paced** segmenting beat learner-paced, and PSI's small mastery-gated modules have a solid meta-analytic record (Kulik et al. 1990). Segmenting into coherent units is right.

Contradiction: "exactly one" makes every session **blocked**, and blocked-vs-interleaved is the largest order-only effect in the review — **d = 0.83 on a one-month-delayed test with identical content** (Rohrer et al. 2020). Also contradicted by the whole-task tradition: 4C/ID's unit is a *task class* containing several related-difficulty variants; Merrill's is a progression of whole tasks. And segmenting costs time (**d = −0.92 on learning time**), so the unit should not be smaller than necessary. Microlearning provides no support for a smaller unit — that literature is too weak to cite **[W]**.

Net: one *focal* concept per session is fine. One concept *exclusively* per session is the problem.

### (d) Frontier-order traversal — **SUPPORTED as a constraint, UNSUPPORTED as a selection rule**

The frontier itself is exactly ALEKS's outer fringe and is validated. What is unsupported is taking **"the first node on the frontier"** as the choice. KST computes the fringe and then *lets the learner choose from it*; nothing in the literature says the first element of an arbitrary topological order is the right pick. Simulation evidence says different valid sequences over the same hierarchy produce different outcomes, with weaker learners hurt most (Instructional Science 2017) — i.e. the choice *within* the frontier is where the (modest) sequencing effect lives, and "first" throws that away.

And the whole-task literature (Reigeluth, Merrill, van Merriënboer) contradicts frontier-first *as the organizing spine*: prerequisite-first traversal guarantees the learner meets N components before ever seeing what they compose into. Note that the whole-task evidence is much weaker **[W/S]** than the KST evidence **[P]** — so this is a design/motivation argument, not an outcome-evidence argument.

### (e) `est_sessions: 1-3` as unit size — **NO EVIDENCE (as an estimate) / CONTRADICTED (as a cap)**

There is no research that licenses "1–3 sessions" as a unit size; nothing in this literature sizes units in sessions at all. The one quantitative anchor is Koedinger et al. (2023): **~7 practice opportunities per knowledge component to reach 80%**, with the variance across students driven by *initial performance*, not learning rate. That is a number about opportunities, not sessions, and it says the required amount **cannot be estimated up front from the concept** — it depends on where the learner starts, which is unknown at goal-creation time.

Used as a *prior*, harmless. Used as a *cap* or as an input to a completion judgement, it is contradicted by the wheel-spinning literature: the modelled failure is precisely "significant practice, no mastery," and a capped estimate will resolve it by declaring done.

### (f) Binary done/not-done status — **CONTRADICTED**

Three independent literatures converge against a terminal latch:

1. **Learning trajectories**: student performance "does not simply increase monotonically. Rather students move back and forth along the trajectory" (CPRE **[P]**); levels are "expected probabilities," and students "advance and fall back" (Confrey **[P]**).
2. **Forgetting/spacing**: retention decays as a function of time since learning — ALEKS measures Ebbinghaus curves *by knowledge-state layer* and re-assesses on a schedule precisely because learned ≠ permanently known **[P]**. Cepeda et al. (2008) on optimal gap scaling.
3. **Spiral curriculum**: a spiral has no terminal state by construction; and the learner-facing criticism (Coelho & Moles, via Woodward **[P]**) is that learners can't tell when a stage is "enough" without knowing the revisit point — which is what a `done` flag falsely promises.

Additional problem: `done` is set by **tutor judgement** rather than by a demonstrated criterion. PSI — the strongest historical evidence for mastery gating (Kulik et al. 1990) — gated on a *mastery test*, not on the tutor's impression. Seba has the harder version of a known problem: an LLM judging its own teaching's success, with no delayed measurement.

## 11. Recommendations, ordered by expected effect

**1. Replace binary `done` with a decaying mastery estimate plus a scheduled revisit.**
Largest expected effect, cheapest change. `status` becomes `{unseen, in-progress, learned}` + `strength` (a scalar) + `last_seen` + `next_review`. `learned` is never terminal; a concept re-enters the eligible set when strength decays below threshold. Justification: CPRE non-monotonicity **[P]**, Confrey "advance and fall back" **[P]**, spacing (Cepeda), ALEKS's scheduled re-assessment **[P]**. This is also what makes the spiral real without adopting spiral rhetoric.

**2. Stop teaching exactly one concept per session; make the session unit a small related *cluster* and interleave practice across it.**
Keep one *focal* concept for new instruction, but draw practice/retrieval from 2–4 related concepts (recent + due). Justification: Rohrer et al. 2020, **d = 0.83 on a delayed test from an order-only manipulation** **[P]** — the largest sequencing effect in this review, and Seba currently implements the losing arm. Also aligns with 4C/ID task classes. Constraint from the literature: interleave *confusable, related* concepts, not arbitrary ones.

**3. Diagnose the entry point before drafting the syllabus, and re-diagnose periodically.**
Koedinger et al. **[P]**: learners differ enormously in *initial* knowledge and barely at all in *rate*. Therefore locating the frontier correctly dominates optimizing the order. Concretely: an adaptive placement pass at goal creation that marks concepts as already-known, plus a periodic re-assessment that can *demote* concepts. This is ALEKS's core loop and it is the single most validated mechanism in the review.

**4. Make the syllabus revisable, with a defined revision trigger.**
Minimum viable version: after each session the tutor may propose (i) split a concept, (ii) insert a missing prerequisite, (iii) mark a concept already-known, (iv) drop a concept as out-of-goal. Surface proposals to the learner in batch, not per-session. Justification: Confrey's 24% item-revision rate **[P]**, "validation is an ongoing process" **[P]**, CPRE's "revised and retried" **[P]**, expert tutors' mid-session policy change **[S]**. The strongest trigger to implement first is (ii) — a missing prerequisite discovered mid-session is the highest-frequency real defect in a TOC-derived DAG.

**5. Add wheel-spinning detection with an escape hatch.**
If a concept has consumed > ~2× its `est_sessions` (or N consecutive sessions without demonstrated progress), do **not** mark it done — escalate: split the concept, insert a prerequisite, switch representation/strategy, or park it and route around. Justification: Zhang et al. 2019 **[P]** — wheel spinning is early-detectable with trivial models, and the entire point of detecting it is to *change strategy*. Without this, `est_sessions: 1-3` plus tutor-judged `done` will silently paper over the exact failure the literature names.

**6. Open the syllabus with an epitome session, and add periodic synthesis sessions.**
Session 1 of a goal: a simplified but *whole* instance of the target performance, before any component teaching. Then every ~5 concepts, a session that re-does a harder whole instance using what's been learned. Justification: Reigeluth's zoom lens / epitome, Merrill's "progression of increasingly complex whole tasks," 4C/ID task classes. Honest caveat: the *evidence* for elaboration theory specifically is weak **[W]** (English & Reigeluth 1996 is n = 13 formative research). The strong argument here is motivational and orienting, not outcome-evidence — but the cost is 1 session in ~6 and the DAG remains intact underneath.

**7. Derive the syllabus backward from a target performance, using the TOC as a coverage checklist rather than as the spine.**
At goal creation, first elicit/state: *what will the learner be able to do, and what would demonstrate it?* Then select from the TOC only what serves it. Justification: Wiggins & McTighe (stage 2 before stage 3); Confrey's warning that LTs differ from "solely logical deconstructions" **[P]**. Evidence for UbD itself is implementation-dependent **[W]**, but this costs one extra prompt at goal creation and prevents inheriting an author's coverage decisions wholesale.

**8. Let the learner choose from the frontier; keep the frontier system-computed.**
Present 2–4 eligible next concepts with one-line rationales; the learner picks, or accepts the default. Justification: this is exactly ALEKS's outer-fringe mechanism **[P]**; achievement cost is ~zero (learner control g = 0.05, Karich et al. **[S]**); and the frontier constraint prevents the specific bad choices learners make (Bjork et al.: preferring blocked, dropping items prematurely **[S]**). Explicitly do **not** let the learner override prerequisites or mark concepts done.

**9. Stop selecting "the first node on the frontier"; add an explicit tie-break.**
Suggested order of criteria: (i) concepts due for review, (ii) concepts that unblock the most downstream nodes, (iii) concepts closest to the stated goal performance, (iv) learner choice. Justification: simulation evidence that different prerequisite-legal sequences produce different outcomes, with lower-ability learners most affected (Instructional Science 2017 **[S]**). Modest expected effect — the empirical literature on topic-order among legal orders is small-to-null — but it is nearly free.

**10. Reinterpret `est_sessions` as a prior with an opportunity budget, and never as a completion criterion.**
Ground it in the one available number: ~7 practice opportunities per KC to reach 80% (Koedinger et al. **[P]**). Track opportunities-and-accuracy, not sessions-elapsed, and let `est_sessions` only set expectations and trigger recommendation 5. Explicitly do not cite microlearning to justify smaller units — that evidence base does not support it **[W]**.

**11. Gate `learned` on a demonstrated criterion, ideally delayed.**
Replace pure tutor judgement with: correct performance on a novel item for the concept, in a session *after* the teaching session. Justification: PSI's meta-analytic success rests on mastery *tests* (Kulik et al. 1990 **[S]**); and the interleaving/spacing literature shows same-session performance systematically overstates durable learning (Rohrer's blocked group looked fine during practice and scored 38% a month later **[P]**). This is the change that most directly reduces the risk of an LLM grading its own teaching.
