# Practitioner Lesson Craft: How Skilled Tutors, Coaches and Course Designers Actually Structure a Session

Scope: the *session frame* — the named, repeatable sequence of phases a working practitioner runs, especially the first five and last five minutes. Learning-science mechanisms (retrieval, spacing, feedback timing), turn-level tutoring moves, curriculum sequencing and learner modelling are covered in earlier rounds and are not re-derived here.

Every claim below is tagged **[EVIDENCE]** (tested against outcomes in a controlled or quasi-controlled study), **[DESCRIPTIVE]** (systematically observed in experts, but not causally tested), or **[CONVENTION]** (what the trade teaches and mandates, on craft authority).

## 1. High-dosage tutoring: the manualised programs

### 1.1 What the meta-analysis actually establishes

Nickow, Oreopoulos & Quan (2020), the first systematic review + meta-analysis of RCT tutoring studies (96 RCTs, PreK–12): pooled effect **0.37 SD**. Teacher and paraprofessional tutors > nonprofessional and parent tutors; in-school > after-school; reading effects strongest in early grades, math in later grades. Notably, **the meta-analysis has almost nothing to say about within-session structure** — it moderates on tutor type, subject, grade, setting, dosage. Session shape is not a coded moderator anywhere in this literature. **[EVIDENCE for dosage/personnel; NO EVIDENCE either way for phase ordering]**
https://www.nber.org/papers/w27476

The design-principles synthesis built on it (Robinson, Kraft, Loeb & Schueler, EdResearch for Recovery Brief #16, Feb 2021) sets the outer envelope:

- **Frequency**: 3+ sessions/week; sessions of **30–60 minutes**; ≥10 weeks, often a full year. "Studies have found little evidence that once-a-week tutoring is sufficient to generate meaningful gains." One meta-analysis found high-dosage tutoring **20× more effective than low-dosage in math, 15× in reading**. **[EVIDENCE]**
- **Group size**: up to 3–4 workable; **1:1 likely most effective**. **[EVIDENCE]**
- **Measurement**: programs supporting data use and ongoing informal assessment let tutors tailor instruction; "for formative assessments to result in more student learning, tutors need time and support to review the assessment." **[EVIDENCE, suggestive]**
- **Relationships**: a *consistent* tutor over time. **[EVIDENCE, suggestive]**
- **Curriculum**: high-quality aligned materials beat ad-hoc help; pure remediation to easier material "found to result only in students falling further behind" — instead address the missed prerequisites **most critical to accessing upcoming content**. **[EVIDENCE]**

https://annenberg.brown.edu/sites/default/files/EdResearch_for_Recovery_Design_Principles_1.pdf · https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED613847.pdf

### 1.2 The program-by-program session protocols

From the appendix "Examples of Effective Tutoring Models" of the same brief — this is the most concrete published comparison of mandated session designs:

| Program | Dose | Ratio | Session internals |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Saga Education** | One ~**50-min class period daily**, during the school day, full year | 2:1 | **First half of each session = skill-building curriculum for remediation; second half tied to what students are learning in their classrooms.** Tutors use formative assessments **"such as exit tickets which allow them to revise the next day's lesson based on the prior day's learning."** Pre/post tests per unit "show tutors how much review time to allow for the first 2–3 weeks of the next unit." |
| **Reading Partners** | Twice-weekly **60-min** sessions, 5 months to over a year | 1:1 | Research-based **scripted** curriculum, 150+ lessons; monitor progress at midyear and end-of-year; **update each student's individual reading plan after the midyear assessment**. |
| **Minnesota Reading Corps** | **20 min every day** (K–3) | 1:1 | "Heavy modeling by tutor, practice by student, and immediate feedback and clear rationale from the tutor." |
| **Minnesota Math Corps** | Two 45-min or three 30-min sessions/week (90 min/wk total) | 2:1 | After intervention on all subskills in a unit, **mastery assessment covering each subskill; any unmastered subskill triggers a remedial lesson**. |
| **Match Corps** | **2 hours/day**, 300+ hrs/yr; for-credit, in-school | 2:1 | "Deliberate, sequenced lesson plans rather than ad-hoc assistance"; tutors do nothing but tutor; weekly supervisor meetings; family contact. |

Saga RCT results (UChicago Education Lab): high-school math **1 to 2.5 years of additional learning in one year**; a blended 4:1 model achieved comparable gains at ~$500/pupil less. Match's Chicago 2:1 math tutorial **cut math course failures by >50%**.
https://educationlab.uchicago.edu/projects/high-dosage-tutoring-research-partnership-with-saga-education/ · https://www.doe.mass.edu/charter/bestpractices/View.aspx?id=81 · https://bellwether.org/blog/match-corps-high-dosage-tutoring-that-works-3/

Three structural facts are directly portable and worth isolating:

1. **Saga splits every session in half** — half on the backlog of prerequisite skills, half on the live current material. This is a *within-session* answer to the review-vs-new tension, not a between-session one.
2. **The exit ticket is not assessment theatre; it is the input to next session's plan.** Saga's protocol is explicitly closed-loop: measure at the end, revise tomorrow's lesson from it.
3. **Pre-test result → amount of review time budgeted** at the start of the next unit. Diagnostic drives dosage.

### 1.3 The canonical session frame (National Student Support Accelerator, Stanford)

The NSSA *Toolkit for Tutoring Programs* publishes an explicit "Example Tutoring Session Structure," the closest thing the field has to a standard. Rationale given: **"When students know what to expect... each session has a consistent rhythm, students will feel safer and more engaged,"** and tutors stop spending prep time on pacing and spend it on content. **[CONVENTION, with the consistency claim asserted rather than tested]**

1. **Session opening: relationship-building.** "Invest significant time at the outset." Check in about the learner's day/week; hobbies, interests.
2. **Data touchpoint.** "Shift the conversation smoothly to a follow-up from the previous session, culminating in an **'entrance ticket'** that assesses the student's current mastery of a relevant skill they learned previously or a new skill." Remediate unfinished learning here if needed.
3. **Framing & objective.** Introduce the focus; **activate relevant prior knowledge with leading questions**; state the objective aloud and **keep a written version visible throughout**.
4. **Mini lesson & explicit model.** Model the step-by-step process. Self-check criteria: "Was my model clear and concise? Was my model aligned with the student practice and the formative assessment? **Did I make all my thinking visible?** Did I place intentional emphasis on the most important step?"
5. **Purposeful independent practice.** "Plenty of time for multiple **'at-bats'**." "Practice should be as independent as possible. **If students get stuck, ask guiding questions; don't provide answers.**"
6. **Formative assessment.** Short; **"ask students to do only what was modeled and practiced"**; typically an **exit ticket**.

https://nssa.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/Toolkit_Session_Structure.pdf

Note the symmetry: **entrance ticket ↔ exit ticket**, and the explicit constraint that the model, the practice and the assessment must all be aligned to the same target.

## 2. Language teaching lesson frames

Four frames dominate teacher training. Their evidential status is uneven and often misrepresented in both directions.

### 2.1 PPP — Presentation, Practice, Production

Present the target form in context → controlled/accuracy practice → freer production. The default coursebook architecture.

- Standard critique: learners build interlanguages and do not acquire form-by-form on demand; the "production" stage frequently fails to elicit the target form at all.
- **The critique has partly reversed.** Anderson (2017), *ELT Journal* 71(2), "A potted history of PPP with the help of ELT Journal": PPP arose *with* communicative language teaching, not from audiolingualism, and given the modern skill-acquisition-theory evidence for explicit instruction plus practice, PPP **"can no longer be rejected as incompatible with research evidence."**
- **Status: CONVENTION with defensible theoretical backing.** No head-to-head RCT establishes PPP as best; the honest reading is that explicit presentation followed by deliberate practice is *compatible* with the evidence, not proven optimal.

https://academic.oup.com/eltj/article/71/2/218/2447419

### 2.2 ESA — Engage, Study, Activate (Harmer)

**Engage** (arouse interest/affect) → **Study** (focus on language form) → **Activate** (use language freely). Harmer's point is that the three elements are *reorderable*: "straight arrows" (E-S-A ≈ PPP), "boomerang" (E-A-S-A — let them try, then teach what broke, then retry), "patchwork" (E-A-A-S-A-S...).

**Status: CONVENTION.** ESA is a teacher-training heuristic; the small comparative studies that exist (e.g. ESA vs PPP on speaking) are underpowered classroom quasi-experiments. What is valuable is the explicit **Engage** slot — no other frame names affect as a mandatory phase — and the boomerang ordering, which is TTT under another name.

https://www.barefootteflteacher.com/p/what-is-engage-study-activate · https://ontesol.com/esl-lesson-planning-frameworks/

### 2.3 TTT — Test, Teach, Test

Diagnostic production task → teach only what the diagnosis exposed → re-test with a parallel task. **Status: CONVENTION**, but it is the frame that most directly implements a defensible principle (teach to the measured gap; measure again immediately). Structurally identical to the boomerang ESA, and to the medical "get a commitment → probe → correct" pattern in §5.

### 2.4 TBLT — Willis's framework

Jane Willis, *A Framework for Task-Based Learning* (1996):

- **Pre-task**: teacher explores the topic, highlights useful words/phrases, learners hear/read a parallel task being done.
- **Task cycle**:
  - *Task* — learners do it in pairs/groups; teacher **"monitors from a distance, encouraging all attempts at communication, not correcting."**
  - *Planning* — learners prepare to report publicly; because it is public they push for accuracy; teacher gives language support here.
  - *Report* — some groups present; teacher comments on content.
- **Language focus**:
  - *Analysis* — learners "examine and then discuss specific features of the text or transcript."
  - *Practice* — drills on the new items that emerged.

Note the inversion versus PPP: **form is addressed last, on evidence of what the learner actually produced**. Compare Saga's exit ticket and the chess "analyse your own game" pattern — all three are *evidence-of-performance-first* designs.

Evidence: Bryfonski & McKay (2019), "TBLT implementation and evaluation: A meta-analysis," *Language Teaching Research* 23(5) — 52 studies, **overall d = 0.93** for TBLT implementation across a range of outcomes. **[EVIDENCE, with a heavy caveat**: this is a meta-analysis of program *implementations and evaluations*, heterogeneous, largely non-randomised, publication-bias-prone, and it does not isolate the pre-task/task/language-focus *ordering* as the active ingredient.]

https://www2.vobs.at/ludescher/grammar/task_based_learning.htm · https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1225836

### 2.5 Dogme / Teaching Unplugged (Thornbury)

Materials-light, conversation-driven, built on **emergent language**: the lesson content is whatever the learners produce; the teacher's job is to notice, upgrade and recycle it, not to deliver a syllabus. Criticisms: demands an expert teacher, weak with low-proficiency learners who cannot yet sustain interaction, poor fit for exam prep, and **research on it is thin**; proponents lean on its resemblance to TBLT for borrowed credibility.

**Status: CONVENTION, and specifically a reaction against frames rather than a frame.** Its usable residue for a 1:1 conversational tutor: the systematic *harvesting* step — during free conversation, silently log the learner's errors and reformulations, then convert them into an explicit focus block, then recycle them in later sessions. That is a card-minting protocol.

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dogme_language_teaching · https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/knowing-subject/teaching-unplugged

### 2.6 Which frame fits a 1:1 conversational tutor

Not PPP: its Practice stage assumes drill machinery and a class. Not pure Dogme: it discards the long-run syllabus a months-long probability/Italian program needs. The fit is **TTT/boomerang-ESA on the outside, PPP on the inside** — open with a production or retrieval task that exposes the gap, teach precisely into the gap (which is where a short PPP micro-cycle lives), then re-task. For Italian this is near-literal; for probability the "task" is a problem attempted before instruction.

## 3. Music instruction — the mature 1:1 tradition

### 3.1 The conventional studio lesson frame

The trade template for a 30–45 min private lesson is: **warm-up → technique (scales/exercises) → repertoire (the main body) → musicianship/theory/ear → assignment for the week, written in a lesson notebook.** Assignment books with fixed categories (warm-ups, technique, repertoire, sight-reading, theory, listening) are a standard commercial product; the written weekly assignment is near-universal. **[CONVENTION]**

Notably, experienced studio teachers push back on rigid time-boxing. A widely-read teacher blog explicitly rejects "Tidy Little Boxes" (warm-ups 3 min / technique 3 min / ear 4 min / repertoire 10 min / …) because it forces teachers to complete components regardless of engagement, and instead recommends varying by season (technique-heavy early year, repertoire-heavy before recitals), by learner, and **abandoning an activity the moment it stops working**. **[CONVENTION, and a useful corrective: fixed *sequence*, flexible *duration*.]**
https://www.teachpianotoday.com/2016/02/11/to-save-my-sanity-i-started-structuring-my-piano-lessons-like-this/ · https://pianoheroes.co/intermediate-piano-lesson-planning/

### 3.2 What expert artist-teachers actually do (systematic observation)

Duke & Simmons (2006), "The Nature of Expertise: Narrative Descriptions of 19 Common Elements Observed in the Lessons of Three Renowned Artist-Teachers," *Bulletin of the Council for Research in Music Education* 170, 7–19. ~25 hours of video of Nelita True (piano), Donald McInnes (viola), Richard Killmer (oboe); 19 elements present in *nearly every lesson by all three*. This is the best-documented account of expert 1:1 instruction in any domain. **[DESCRIPTIVE — observed in experts, not causally tested]**

Elements most relevant to session structure:

**Goals and Expectations**
- "The repertoire assigned students is well within their technical capabilities; no student is struggling with the notes of the piece." Students arrive **having already learned the notes and formed interpretive decisions** — "It is from this point... that work in the lesson begins." *The lesson does not spend its time on what the learner could have done alone.*
- "Teachers have a clear auditory image of the piece that guides their judgments" — the expert holds an explicit target standard before the learner performs.
- "The teachers select lesson targets (i.e., proximal performance goals) that are technically or musically important" — trivial momentary errors are ignored; fundamental issues are "attended to immediately and pursued assiduously."
- **"Lesson targets are positioned at a level of difficulty that is close enough to the student's current skill level that the targets are achievable in the short term and change is audible to the student in the moment."**
- **"The teachers clearly remember students' work in past lessons and frequently draw comparisons between present and past, pointing out both positive and negative differences."** Time spent describing improvement over weeks/months is "notable for its contrast with negative feedback, which is generally pointed and brief."

**Effecting Change**
- "Pieces are performed from beginning to end; in this sense, the lessons are like performances... nearly all playing is judged by a high standard, 'as if we are performing.'" — i.e. **the session opens with a full performance attempt, not with fragments.**
- "In general, the course of the music directs the lesson; errors in student performance elicit stops." The *learner's own errors are the agenda*.
- "The teachers are tenacious in working to accomplish lesson targets, having students repeat target passages until performance is accurate... They **do not give up or simply tell students to 'go practice.'"**
- "Lessons proceed at an intense, rapid pace," with **very brief teacher episodes** and rapid alternation between teacher and student activity, "interrupted from time to time with what seem to be 'intuitively timed' breaks, during which the teachers give an extended demonstration or tell a story" — deliberate pace-breaking for relaxation, then straight back to intensity.

**Conveying Information**
- "Negative feedback is clear, pointed, frequent and directed at very specific aspects"; **its frequency is markedly higher than positive feedback**. Positive feedback is "infrequent, intermittent, unexpected... but... of high magnitude and extended duration," occurring at least once in nearly every lesson and unmistakably differentiated.
- Teachers model by playing excerpts from the student's own repertoire, often juxtaposing **a faithful imitation of the student's version against the target version**.

https://www.jstor.org/stable/40319345 · https://woodwindshelp.weebly.com/uploads/2/3/7/9/23791000/common_elements_observed.pdf · https://cml.music.utexas.edu/online-resources/the-nature-of-expertise

### 3.3 The practice assignment — what to tell them to do this week

Duke, Simmons & Cash (2009), "It's Not How Much; It's How: Characteristics of Practice Behavior and Retention of Performance Skills," *Journal of Research in Music Education* 56(4). 17 advanced pianists practising a 3-bar Shostakovich passage; **practice duration and repetition count did not predict retention performance — practice *strategies* did.** Top performers: errors were corrected immediately and at the point of the error; practice was interrupted the moment an error appeared; the target passage was rehearsed at varied tempi; errors were not allowed to be repeated. **[EVIDENCE]**

Direct implication for a lesson hand-off: **"practise this for 30 minutes" is the wrong assignment; "practise these two bars, stopping at each error, until you can play them three times clean at 60bpm" is the right one.** The assignment must specify the *procedure*, not the *quantity*.

## 4. Athletic and skill coaching

### 4.1 The conventional session shape

The near-universal four-part coaching session: **warm-up → technical/skill practice → small-sided games / application → cool-down (+ review).** US Soccer's curriculum organises sessions in three blocks; grassroots frameworks add free play. **[CONVENTION]**
https://www.ussoccer.com/soccer-forward/resource-hub/coaches-session-plans · https://footballdna.co.uk/planning-structuring-coaching-sessions/

**RAMP** (Ian Jeffreys, *Gamespeed*) has largely replaced generic stretching as the warm-up protocol: **R**aise (temperature, HR, blood flow via sport-specific movement) → **A**ctivate & **M**obilise (target the muscle groups and ranges the session will demand) → **P**otentiate (progressively intense, sport-specific work that primes the main session). **[EVIDENCE for the components — post-activation potentiation and against static-stretch-only warm-ups; CONVENTION for the acronym as a package]**
https://www.dsmstrength.com/post/ramp-warm-up-guide-the-complete-warm-up-system

The load-bearing idea in RAMP is that **the warm-up is not separate from the session — it is a low-intensity rehearsal of exactly what the session will demand.** That is a stronger design than "do something easy first."

### 4.2 Constraints-led approach (Davids, Button, Bennett; Renshaw et al.)

Skill emerges from the interaction of **task, environment and performer constraints**; the coach's job is to *design the constraint*, not prescribe the movement. Practically: no isolated drills detached from the game, heavy use of representative small-sided games, manipulate one constraint (pitch size, touch limit, rule) to make the desired behaviour the most attractive solution, and **let the learner search for the solution rather than being told it**. Feedback is minimised and made external-focus.

**Status: strong theoretical base (ecological dynamics), growing but contested empirical support**; the "constraints-led beats traditional" comparisons are mostly small and domain-bound. **[DESCRIPTIVE/CONTESTED]**
https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331783442_The_Constraints-Led_Approach_Principles_for_Sports_Coaching_and_Practice_Design · https://perceptionaction.com/cla/

### 4.3 On ordering

There is real evidence on *ordering* here that generalises: the blocked-vs-random and constant-vs-variable practice literature (Shea & Morgan; Schmidt & Bjork's "desirable difficulties") shows blocked practice produces better within-session performance and **worse retention and transfer** than interleaved/variable practice. This is the strongest ordering evidence in any coaching tradition and it cuts against the intuitive "master this, then move on" progression inside a session. **[EVIDENCE]** (Covered in the learning-science round; flagged here because it directly contradicts the conventional coaching block structure.)

## 5. Medical education: the two portable 1:1 micro-frames

These are the most refined short 1:1 teaching protocols in existence, designed for encounters of 5–10 minutes, and both are directly transplantable.

### 5.1 One-Minute Preceptor (Neher, Gordon, Meyer & Stevens 1992) — teacher-led

The steps, verbatim from the MAHEC public-domain monograph:

1. **Get a commitment.** After the learner presents, before any teaching: "What do you think is going on with this patient?" The point is not more data — it is to force the learner to *state a position*. "The act of stating a commitment pushes the learner to move beyond their level of comfort and makes the teaching encounter more active and more personal." Variants: "What other diagnoses would you consider?", "How do you think we should treat this?"
2. **Probe for supporting evidence.** *Resist the urge to pass immediate judgement.* "What factors in the history and physical support your diagnosis?" — this separates a lucky guess from sound reasoning and exposes the actual reasoning chain.
3. **Reinforce what was done well.** Specific behaviours, never "that was a good presentation": "Your diagnosis of probable pneumonia was well supported by your history and physical."
4. **Give guidance about errors and omissions.** Specific, alternative-providing, avoiding "bad"/"poor" in favour of "not best"/"it is preferred."
5. **Teach a general principle.** Generalise the case to a transferable rule — the anti-overfitting step. "Smokers are more likely than non-smokers to be infected with gram-negative organisms..."
6. **Conclusion** (close the encounter, state next steps).

Ordering logic: **commitment before correction, evidence before evaluation, specific praise before specific correction, and generalisation last.**

**Evidence**: Furney et al. (2001), "Teaching the One-Minute Preceptor: a randomized controlled trial," *J Gen Intern Med* 16(9):620–4. 57 internal-medicine residents (28 intervention / 29 control), 1-hour intervention. Learners rated intervention residents significantly higher on **asking for a commitment**, **providing feedback** (positive feedback, explaining reasoning, offering suggestions, frequency) and **motivating outside reading**. **"Teaching a general principle" did not improve** (attributed to residents lacking developed teaching scripts). Overall global teaching-effectiveness ratings did not differ. Conclusion: "modest improvements." **[EVIDENCE — real but modest; the frame changes teacher behaviour more reliably than it changes global perceived quality]**
https://www.gvsu.edu/cms4/asset/E6494549-9D1E-60EB-2FAF608662526253/the_one_minute_preceptor.pdf · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1495264/

### 5.2 SNAPPS (Wolpaw, Wolpaw & Papp 2003, *Academic Medicine* 78:893–898) — learner-led

The mirror image of OMP: the *learner* runs the six steps and the teacher facilitates. "The learner can and should be taught to lead."

1. **S**ummarize briefly the history and findings. Constrained: **"should not occupy more than 50% of the learning encounter and, generally, should be no longer than three minutes,"** presented at a higher level of abstraction (semantic qualifiers: "yesterday" → "acute").
2. **N**arrow the differential to two or three relevant possibilities. Commit *before* the teacher engages.
3. **A**nalyze the differential by comparing and contrasting the possibilities. This is the articulation step — verbalise the discriminating reasoning, not the conclusion.
4. **P**robe the preceptor by asking questions about uncertainties, difficulties, or alternative approaches. **The learner is expected to reveal confusion and knowledge deficits and is *rewarded* for doing so.** "This step is the most unique aspect of the learner-driven model."
5. **P**lan management for the patient's medical issues.
6. **S**elect a case-related issue for self-directed learning. The learner names one focused question to read about, checks it with the teacher to sharpen it, **keeps it on an index card, reads soon after ("in a regular, disciplined, patient-based manner rather than in long, unfocused bursts"), and brings it back to the next encounter to probe the preceptor with follow-up questions.**

Whole encounter: presentations "should generally not exceed six to seven minutes."

**Evidence**: Wolpaw et al. (2009), "Using SNAPPS to Facilitate the Expression of Clinical Reasoning and Uncertainties: A Randomized Comparison Group Trial," *Academic Medicine* 84(4) — SNAPPS **increased expressed diagnostic reasoning and expressed uncertainties without lengthening presentations**. A 2024 systematic review of SNAPPS in clinical-reasoning teaching exists (PMID 38446132). **[EVIDENCE for process outcomes; downstream learning outcomes not established]**
https://www.med.umn.edu/sites/med.umn.edu/files/snapps_article.pdf · https://journals.lww.com/academicmedicine/fulltext/2009/04000/Using_SNAPPS_to_Facilitate_the_Expression_of.00032.aspx

**Step 6 is the single most transplantable closing move found in this entire review.** It is a learner-selected, tutor-sharpened, written-down, deliberately-scoped question that becomes the explicit opening of the following session. That is exactly Seba's "next-session hint + card minting" slot, already validated as feasible in a domain where the teacher has ~90 seconds.

## 6. Cognitive apprenticeship (Collins, Brown & Newman; Collins, Brown & Holum 1991)

Both a theory and a session-structure model. The six teaching methods, in their intended order of deployment:

1. **Modeling** — "an expert's performing a task so that the students can observe and build a conceptual model," with the internal reasoning externalised.
2. **Coaching** — "observing students while they carry out a task and offering hints, scaffolding, feedback, modeling, reminders, and new tasks."
3. **Scaffolding** — "the supports the teacher provides to help the student carry out the task," progressively **faded**.
4. **Articulation** — "any method of getting students to articulate their knowledge, reasoning, or problem-solving processes."
5. **Reflection** — "enabling students to compare their own problem-solving processes with those of an expert, another student, and ultimately, an internal cognitive model," often via **replay** of the learner's own performance with critical features highlighted.
6. **Exploration** — "pushing students into a mode of problem solving on their own," formulating their own questions.

Content dimension: domain knowledge / heuristic strategies / **control (metacognitive) strategies** / learning strategies — the last two are what the frame uniquely insists on teaching explicitly.

Sequencing principles: **global before local** ("build a conceptual map... before attending to the details"), **increasing complexity**, **increasing diversity** (widening the variety of strategies required — i.e. interleaving).

**Status: DESCRIPTIVE/THEORETICAL.** It is a synthesis of three successful instructional programs (Palincsar & Brown's reciprocal teaching, Scardamalia & Bereiter's procedural facilitation, Schoenfeld's mathematical problem solving), each of which has its own evidence; the six-method sequence as a package has not been tested against alternatives. Its distinctive contribution to session design is that **modelling→coaching→fading→articulation→reflection→exploration is a trajectory across many sessions, not a single-session agenda** — and that reflection is operationalised as *replaying the learner's own process*.
https://www.aft.org/ae/winter1991/collins_brown_holum

## 7. Chess and expertise coaching: the session built on the learner's own errors

Chess coaching converges on one structure, and it is the purest form of error-centred teaching:

**Analysing your own games — the mandated order** (chesscoachroeland.com, representative of the standard method):
1. **Replay the whole game alone, engine off**, marking critical moments — where you were uncertain, where you spent a lot of time, where momentum shifted. "Not every move needs deep examination and therefore we make a selection."
2. **Analyse those positions deeply, still without the engine**, writing down your reasoning, questions and candidate variations.
3. **Only then turn on the engine** and compare against your written analysis.
4. **Write 2–4 practical takeaways**, accumulating "a personal training database tailored to your strengths and weaknesses."

**Status: CONVENTION**, essentially universal among coaches. But it encodes a testable and well-supported principle: **generate your own answer and commit it in writing before seeing the authoritative answer** (a pretesting / generation-effect structure). The engine here plays exactly the role an LLM tutor is tempted to play — and the tradition's hard-won rule is *don't consult it first*.

Weekly coach structure (representative): tactics via spaced repetition (30–45 min) → annotated master games, pausing at critical positions to self-evaluate before revealing → endgames → sparring games analysed engine-free first. Errors are **tagged by phase** (opening / middlegame / endgame / time management) and tracked as metrics (accuracy %, blunders per game); when a metric plateaus for two weeks, the coach re-points the practice at that weakness. **[CONVENTION, but note the explicit error-taxonomy + plateau-triggered replanning loop.]**
https://www.chesscoachroeland.com/post/how-to-analyze-your-own-chess-games-correctly · https://plychess.com/blog/coach-chess-training-methods

## 8. Course and lecture design at scale

**Segment length.** Guo, Kim & Rubin (2014), "How video production affects student engagement: an empirical study of MOOC videos," *L@S '14* — 6.9M video-watching sessions across four edX courses. The headline finding: **median engagement time drops sharply past ~6 minutes**; engagement is roughly flat under 6 min and falls off steeply after, with videos over 12 min showing very low completion. Other findings: informal talking-head + Khan-style handwriting beat slides alone; **pre-produced polished studio lectures underperformed more personal/spontaneous delivery**; faster, higher-energy speaking correlated with higher engagement. **[EVIDENCE — but observational, and confounded: long videos are often long *because* they are dull, and viewers self-select.]**
https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2556325.2566239 · http://learningatscale.acm.org/las2014/talks/paper_philip_guo2.pdf

The safe extraction is not "6 minutes is magic," it is **chunk exposition and break it with interaction**; the 6-minute figure is a reasonable default ceiling for uninterrupted delivery.

This corroborates, from a completely different direction, Duke & Simmons's observation that expert music teachers' own speaking turns are "generally very brief" with rapid alternation between teacher and learner activity.

## 9. How expert tutors open and close: the first move and the last move

### 9.1 Lepper's recurrent phase sequence

Lepper & Woolverton, "The wisdom of practice: Lessons learned from the study of highly effective tutors" (in Aronson, ed., *Improving Academic Achievement*, 2002), studying expert vs mediocre maths tutors. Their central structural finding: **"Most of the expert/effective tutoring followed a recurrent sequence of phases as students worked through a series of problems":**

1. **Introduction** (tutor and student become acquainted)
2. **Problem selection** (tutor)
3. **Problem presentation** (tutor)
4. **Problem solution** (mainly tutee)
5. **Reflection** (tutor — encourage thought about solutions, implications, etc.)
6. **Instruction** (when necessary — direct, for problems)

Note steps 5 and 6: **reflection precedes direct instruction, and instruction is conditional.** Also note that the tutor selects the problem — the expert does not ask the learner what they want to work on.

Expert tutors run **two diagnostic models simultaneously — cognitive and motivational** — and the hard cases are where the two point in different directions.

The INSPIRE characteristics with their concrete behaviours (from the same source, UBC CWSEI summary):
- **Intelligent** — knows which problems will be hard, **"know what types of errors are most likely,"** knows which problems *appear* harder than they are.
- **Nurturant** — **"establish personal rapport early"**; empathise with difficulties; show confidence in the student's ability to succeed.
- **Socratic, not didactic** — **"more than 90% of remarks are in the form of questions"**; hints not answers; **"persist with many sequential hints, starting with general ones, and becoming more specific as necessary."** Distinguishes **productive errors** (deliberately allowed to occur so they can be "systematically debuggged") from **nonproductive errors** (detected and intervened on early). "Ignore small errors when they don't prevent arrival at a correct answer."
- **Progressive** — start with problems that **diagnose** initial knowledge and misunderstanding; subsequent problems selected to correct the misunderstandings found, before moving on. **"Progressive routines: effectively structure tutoring sessions using recurring routines, helping focus students' attention on appropriate issues at different phases of the tutorial."**
- **Indirect** — negative feedback delivered as questions that imply an error's existence and sometimes its location, prompting the student to catch their own; **less likely to praise explicitly**, especially person-directed praise.
- **Reflective** — **"have students reflect aloud immediately after a successful problem solution"**; **"have student keep a written list in their own words of general 'lessons' they had learned"**; periodically ask for explanations; ask students to relate the work to other problem types or real situations.
- **Encouraging** — bolster competence; **emphasise a problem's difficulty in advance** (gives an excuse for failure and raises the value of success); pique curiosity; **"ask students to predict similarities or differences between current and previous problems"**; deliberately highlight inconsistencies to provoke resolution; offer choices and protect the sense of agency.

**Status: DESCRIPTIVE** (observational comparison of expert vs less-effective tutors), but unusually operational.
https://www.eoas.ubc.ca/research/cwsei/resources/INSPIRE-Guidelines.pdf · https://stafforini.com/works/lepper-2002-wisdom-practice-lessons/

### 9.2 The five-step tutoring dialogue frame

Graesser & Person's corpus analysis of naturalistic tutoring identified a "pervasive discourse pattern," the **five-step dialogue frame**, later the backbone of AutoTutor:

> "(1) tutor asks question, (2) student answers question, (3) tutor gives short feedback on answer quality, (4) tutor and student collaboratively improve on answer quality, and (5) tutor assesses the student's understanding."

Step 4 — **collaborative improvement of the answer** — is the step untrained tutors skip and the step that carries the pedagogical load. Step 5 is a *verification* move at the exit of each topic, i.e. a micro-exit-ticket per concept rather than only per session.
https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED361402.pdf · https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA266199.pdf · https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1389041799000054

### 9.3 What the traditions agree on for the first 5 and last 5 minutes

**First five minutes** — every manualised tradition puts *two* things here, in this order:
- **Relational reconnection** (NSSA: "invest significant time at the outset"; Lepper: "establish personal rapport early"; Lepper's phase 1 "Introduction"). **[CONVENTION, plus indirect EVIDENCE via the relationships design principle]**
- **A retrieval/diagnostic probe over prior material** before any new content — NSSA's *entrance ticket*, TTT's first Test, the coaching warm-up-as-rehearsal, music's "play the piece through as if performing," chess's tactics warm-up. **[EVIDENCE for retrieval practice generally; CONVENTION for placing it first]**

Nobody opens with new content. Not one tradition.

**Last five minutes** — three things recur:
- **A short aligned check** on exactly what was modelled and practised (exit ticket; five-step frame step 5). **[CONVENTION, and mechanically important: Saga uses it as the input to the next plan]**
- **Generalisation** — lift the specific case to a transferable rule (OMP step 5 "teach a general principle"; Lepper's "written list in their own words of general lessons"; chess's "2–4 practical takeaways"; cognitive apprenticeship's reflection). **[CONVENTION, converging across four unrelated traditions]**
- **A procedurally specified assignment / self-directed learning issue** (music's written weekly assignment; SNAPPS step 6's index card; chess's personal training database). Duke/Simmons/Cash makes the sharp version of this: specify the *method*, not the *duration*. **[EVIDENCE for the method-over-duration point]**

## 11. Recommendations for Seba's session shape

Ordered by expected value. A 30–60 min text session, one adult learner, months-long, probability + Italian.

1. **Open every session with a retrieval probe on prior material, never with new content — an "entrance ticket."** [EVIDENCE for retrieval; CONVENTION for the placement, but the placement is unanimous across every tradition surveyed.] 2–5 items, drawn from due cards, answered before Seba says anything instructional. It doubles as the session's diagnostic and as spaced review, so it costs no extra time.

2. **Close every session by writing the exit state into the next session's plan — make the exit ticket mechanically consequential.** [EVIDENCE — this is Saga's documented protocol: "exit tickets which allow them to revise the next day's lesson based on the prior day's learning," and Minnesota Math Corps's unmastered-subskill → auto-remedial-lesson rule.] The exit check must cover *only what was modelled and practised in that session* (NSSA). If Seba already mints cards at session end, the missing half is the *plan delta*: what tomorrow starts with, and how much review time to budget.

3. **Adopt SNAPPS step 6 verbatim as the last move: the learner selects one focused question for between-session work, Seba sharpens its scope, it is written down, and the next session opens by returning to it.** [EVIDENCE for process outcomes; and it is the closest published analogue to Seba's next-session hint.] Critically, the *learner* proposes it and the tutor narrows it — not the reverse. Seba should refuse an overbroad one ("review conditional probability") and negotiate it down to a probe-sized question.

4. **Make the hand-off specify a procedure, not a quantity.** [EVIDENCE — Duke, Simmons & Cash 2009: practice duration and repetition count did not predict retention; strategy did.] "Do 20 minutes of Italian" is the wrong assignment; "read this paragraph aloud, stop at every word you hesitate on, and write those five words down" is the right one. Same for probability: name the stopping rule and the error-handling rule, not the time.

5. **Structure the body as attempt-first: extract a commitment before teaching.** [EVIDENCE — OMP's "get a commitment" was the microskill that improved most in Furney's RCT; SNAPPS's "narrow to 2–3 before engaging the preceptor" is the same move; TTT, TBLT, Lepper and chess all do it.] For probability: the learner states an approach and a guess before Seba confirms anything. For Italian: the learner produces before the form is presented. This is also the correct defence against an LLM's default failure mode — answering first.

6. **Then probe the reasoning before evaluating it.** [EVIDENCE, OMP step 2 — "resist the urge to pass immediate judgement"; the explicit purpose is to distinguish a lucky guess from sound reasoning.] For a text tutor this is one extra turn — "what made you pick that?" — before any correctness signal, and it is the cheapest high-value move on this list.

7. **Split the session explicitly, Saga-style: first block on the backlog of prerequisites, second block on the current frontier.** [EVIDENCE — this is Saga's mandated 50-minute structure and Saga is the strongest-evidenced 1:1-ish program in the corpus.] For Seba this maps onto: due-card review and prerequisite repair first, new/current material second. It resolves review-vs-new inside a session instead of alternating sessions.

8. **Name one proximal target per session and hold the learner to it until change is visible in-session.** [DESCRIPTIVE — Duke & Simmons: targets "positioned at a level of difficulty that is close enough to the student's current skill level that the targets are achievable in the short term and **change is audible to the student in the moment**"; experts "do not give up or simply tell students to 'go practice.'"] Seba should not end a session having identified a weakness and deferred it. Also: state the target explicitly and keep it visible (NSSA keeps it written and on display throughout).

9. **End with an explicit generalisation move: lift the session's specifics to a stated rule, in the learner's own words.** [CONVENTION, but it converges independently across OMP step 5, Lepper's "written list in their own words of general lessons," chess's "2–4 practical takeaways," and cognitive apprenticeship's reflection.] Caveat worth stating: it was the *only* OMP microskill that failed to improve in Furney's RCT, because it demands a stored teaching script — which is precisely the thing an LLM tutor has in abundance and a novice human tutor lacks. This is a step where Seba structurally outperforms a human novice.

10. **Cap uninterrupted exposition and alternate rapidly.** [EVIDENCE, weakly — Guo et al.: engagement drops sharply past ~6 min of uninterrupted video; DESCRIPTIVE — Duke & Simmons: expert teacher episodes "generally very brief," rapid teacher/student alternation.] In text terms: no multi-screen explanations; break every explanation with a question the learner must answer. A message the learner scrolls past is a 12-minute video.

11. **Use graduated hints, not answers — general first, then progressively specific.** [DESCRIPTIVE — Lepper: >90% of expert remarks are questions; "persist with many sequential hints, starting with general ones, and becoming more specific as necessary"; NSSA: "if students get stuck, ask guiding questions; don't provide answers."] Seba likely already does some of this; the operational addition is the *persistence* — experts do not collapse to the answer after one failed hint.

12. **Triage errors: ignore small ones that don't block the answer; allow productive errors to run so they can be debugged; intervene early only on nonproductive ones.** [DESCRIPTIVE — Lepper's productive/nonproductive error distinction; Duke & Simmons: "more trivial issues, like intermittent, momentary errors, tend to be ignored," while fundamental technical flaws are addressed with "utmost priority."] A tutor that corrects everything is a worse tutor, and an LLM's default is to correct everything. This needs an explicit suppression rule.

13. **Invert the feedback ratio: make corrective feedback frequent, specific and brief; make praise rare, unexpected, and large when it comes.** [DESCRIPTIVE — Duke & Simmons: negative feedback frequency "markedly higher" than positive; positive feedback "infrequent, intermittent, unexpected, but... of high magnitude and extended duration," at least once in nearly every lesson; Lepper: experts are "less likely to provide explicit praise... especially praise directed at the person."] This is the sharpest contradiction between expert practice and default LLM behaviour in this entire review.

14. **Reconnect relationally in the first minutes, and reference the learner's own past sessions concretely.** [CONVENTION for the opening chat; DESCRIPTIVE for the continuity — Duke & Simmons: experts "clearly remember students' work in past lessons and frequently draw comparisons between present and past," spending considerable time describing improvement over weeks and months.] For Seba this is a memory feature, not a warmth feature: "three weeks ago you were reaching for the wrong tense here and you didn't this time" is the move, and it is the one thing a persistent AI tutor can do better than a human who sees the learner weekly.

15. **For Italian specifically, run TTT/boomerang-ESA on the outside with a PPP micro-cycle inside.** [CONVENTION; TBLT's d = 0.93 is real but too heterogeneous to lean on hard, and PPP is defensible-but-unproven.] Open with a communicative task, harvest the errors and reformulations from it (the usable residue of Dogme), teach precisely into the harvested gap, then re-task. The harvest is also the card-minting source — mint from the learner's own production, not from a syllabus list.

16. **For probability, run the chess protocol on the learner's own worked errors: learner re-derives their failed solution unaided and in writing before Seba shows the correct one.** [CONVENTION in chess, but it instantiates generation/pretesting effects.] The explicit rule from the chess tradition — *engine last* — is the one worth importing: Seba withholds the authoritative solution until the learner has committed a written attempt and named where they think it broke.

17. **Keep the phase sequence fixed and the phase durations flexible.** [CONVENTION on both sides — NSSA argues consistent rhythm makes learners "feel safer and more engaged"; experienced studio teachers reject rigid time-boxing and abandon an activity the moment it stops working.] These are reconcilable and the reconciliation is the recommendation: same phases in the same order every session, no minute budgets.

18. **Do not model first by default.** [DESCRIPTIVE, from the balance of traditions.] The attempt-first camp — TTT, TBLT, OMP, SNAPPS, Lepper, chess, constraints-led — contains every 1:1 expert-observation study and both RCT-tested micro-frames; the model-first camp (PPP, NSSA, cognitive apprenticeship) is largely classroom- and novice-tutor-oriented, where a model is needed because the learner cannot yet attempt anything. An adult learner in month three can attempt. Model *after* the attempt, against the attempt — as Duke & Simmons's experts do, juxtaposing an imitation of the student's version with the target version.

### What is genuinely unknown

No study in this corpus tests session *phase ordering* against outcomes. The tutoring meta-analyses moderate on dosage, personnel and setting and are silent on structure. Every phase-ordering claim above is either a transplanted mechanism (retrieval, generation, desirable difficulties) or expert convention. That is not a reason to ignore it — the convergence across seven unrelated traditions on "diagnose first, generalise and hand off last" is itself informative — but it should not be reported as if the frames themselves had been tested. They have not.
